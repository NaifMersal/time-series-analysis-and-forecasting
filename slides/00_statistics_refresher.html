A. Randomness has a shape

The random variable, the distribution, and the two numbers that summarize it

Before it is measured, a height is a random variable

\(Y\): the height of the next man you measure: not yet known

\(y\): the same height, once measured: a number

A random variable is the whole set of values \(Y\) could take, before it is seen. Statistics is the study of that set.

Delivery mode only. The capital and lowercase distinction is the one that trips people later, so give it a sentence. The next slide names the sample every picture in this deck is drawn on.

Every picture here is drawn on the same 799 heights

Before it is anything else it is a list of 799 numbers, and every idea in this deck is a question asked of it.

NHANES I Epidemiologic Follow-up Study · where these numbers come from

Delivery mode only. The survey is the NHANES follow-up study, and the heights are measured to a fraction of a centimetre. That matters more than it sounds: a sample rounded to the inch piles onto a lattice, and segment D’s test then rejects it on the rounding alone. One sample, five questions: what shape, cut where, one population or two, and what a test of the claim says.

A distribution is the shape of the data

A distribution is the full set of plausible values and how often each occurs. Its picture is a histogram; the mean is one number inside it.

Delivery mode only. Same numbers as the previous slide, the pile tipped into bins. The histogram is the distribution made visible. The next slide puts two numbers on it.

The mean is the centre, the SD is the width

\[\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i\]

\[s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}\]

\(\bar{y}\) is 175.3 cm — the centre. \(s\) is 6.9 cm — the typical distance from it. \(s^2\) is the variance: that width squared.

Delivery mode only. Read the picture before the formulas: the SD is a width, and the shaded bands and the shares they cover are what makes it one. Then the formulas, left to right in words, because the course writes both, and the mean before the SD because the SD is built out of it. The bar means averaged and the hat, two segments from now, means estimated. If anyone asks why the SD divides by n-1 rather than n: the deviations are measured from the sample’s own mean rather than the true one, so they come out slightly too small, and the smaller divisor puts that back. A tall person is not an error, they are the tail of the shape. Say the variance out loud even though nothing here uses it: on Day 1 independent errors add variances rather than SDs, and that is where the square comes back.

The bell curve

\[Y \sim N(\mu,\ \sigma^2)\]

  • A bell puts 68% of its values inside one SD, and 95% inside two
  • These men put 68% and 96% there: close, and measured

Delivery mode only. Two numbers, one whole shape. The 68% and 95% are shares of all the data — a share has a name of its own, which is the next segment. The gap between the histogram and the curve is the price of the assumption, and it is visible here rather than asserted. Segment D comes back and tests this claim properly. The one line of notation is the line every model in the course is written in, so read it out: Y is distributed as a normal with centre mu and width sigma. Greek because the pair describes the bell rather than any sample, which is the distinction segment D sharpens. The squared sigma inside the bracket is the variance from the last slide.

A probability is a share of all possible values

\(P(\text{something})\) is the share of all possible values where that something is true, a number between 0 and 1.

Notation In words
\(P(Y \le 180)\) the next man measures 180 cm or less 0.76
\(P(Y > 180)\) he measures more 0.24
\(P(\lvert Y - \bar{y} \rvert \le s)\) he lands within one SD of the centre 0.68

Capital \(Y\) is doing the work it was introduced for: a probability is a question about the value before it is measured.

Delivery mode only. Nothing here is new arithmetic, it is a name and a bracket for shares the last two slides already read off the picture: the third row is that same 68%, counted off these men. Say that the two rows either side of 180 cm sum to 1, because a probability is a share of one whole pile. This slide exists because segment E argues about this notation, and the misreadings there are about what is inside the bracket.

B. Cutting a distribution

The median, the quantile, and what an 80% range is

The median is the cut that leaves half on each side

The median is a position in a sorted list, not a formula. Sort the 799 heights: the median is number 400, at 175 cm — half below, half above.

Delivery mode only. Start from the median because the room already owns it. Left panel is the sorted list, right panel the same numbers as a histogram. The next slide adds one knob to the same picture.

A quantile is the same cut, with a knob

  • The \(0.5\) quantile is the median: position 400 of 799
  • The \(0.9\) quantile is position 719, the height 184 cm
  • The \(0.1\) quantile is position 80, the height 167 cm

Delivery mode only. One rule, one number: a quantile is a position in a sorted list, and the number on the front is the knob. Same picture as the previous slide with the pair of cuts added, so the knob is the only thing that changed. Quantile and percentile are one object with two names, so do not spend time on the distinction. Say “half the list”, not “half the range”: on a skewed sample the midpoint of the range is nowhere near the median. The next slide hands the knob to the room.

An 80% range is a pair of cuts

An 80% range leaves 10% in each tail, so its ends are the \(0.1\) and \(0.9\) quantiles: 167 cm and 184 cm here. A 95% range leaves 2.5% in each tail, so its ends are the \(0.025\) and \(0.975\) quantiles, 161 cm and 188 cm. Same rule, different knob.

Every interval the course later puts around a forecast is a pair of cuts like these — and on a skewed sample the two cuts are not symmetric around the mean.

Delivery mode only. This is the slide deck 3’s interval segment and deck 4’s CRPS both assume. Worth saying out loud that the two cuts need not be symmetric around the mean, and on a skewed sample they are not.

A cut describes one sample

A distribution tells you the shape of one sample. It does not tell you whether the next value comes from that same shape, or whether the sample was collected in a way that lets you ask. That assumption has a name.

Delivery mode only. The gap: every method in the course that reuses past values assumes the next one is drawn from the same distribution, and that assumption has a name. The next segment says it.

C. i.i.d.

Independent, then identically distributed, then each one breaking on this survey

i.i.d. is two claims

Independent

Each value tells you nothing about the other ones.

Identically distributed

Every value in the sample comes from the same distribution.

They are separate claims, and each breaks its own way: one careless survey breaks the first, a different careless survey breaks the second.

Delivery mode only. The two claims are the whole segment. The next two slides take independence apart; the two after that take identical distribution apart. Do not conflate them: the deck’s whole i.i.d. story is that a sample can satisfy one and fail the other.

Independent means one says nothing about the other

605 pairs of men from different families. If the two are independent the cloud has no tilt, and the correlation sits at -0.02 on a scale from \(-1\) to \(+1\).

Now pair each man with his own brother instead. What happens to the cloud?

Delivery mode only. These are real men out of Galton’s 1886 study, paired at random across families. The scatter is the definition made visible, and the clean case is seen before the broken one. The next slide keeps the same men and the same number of pairs, and changes only who is paired with whom.

Pair each man with his own brother instead

  • The same 605 pairs, now correlating at +0.39
  • Read one brother’s height and you can guess the other’s

Independence fails when one value carries information about another. Same men, same histogram, different pairing.

Galton (1886), 934 children in 205 families · where these numbers come from

Delivery mode only. The tilt is the failure, and the last sentence is the part that matters: a broken independence claim is invisible in a histogram, which is why it needs its own picture. This is Galton’s own finding, and the deck is using it for the reason he collected it. On Day 1 the pair stops being two brothers and becomes this month and last month.

Identically distributed means one shape throughout

Both halves sit at the same level with the same spread, so one distribution stands for all of them — and the histogram has one hump.

Now split the survey by something that is not arbitrary.

Delivery mode only. Two panels, one claim: the boxes are level, so a single mean and a single SD are honest for the whole sample. The split is arbitrary on purpose, because any split of one population gives this picture. The next slide is the same figure on a sample that holds two.

One survey, two populations

The same survey also measured 830 women. Two overlapping populations, and a pooled SD of 9.1 cm against 6.9 and 5.8 inside the groups.

Identical distribution fails when one sample holds two populations. Its pooled 80% range, 157 to 181 cm, is right for nobody.

Delivery mode only. Nothing is wrong with either group; what is wrong is one bell standing for both. This is the failure the course pays for later: on Day 2 a band built from every error a model ever made is meant to cover 80% and covers 61%, for exactly this reason. A subtler real version sits in the same file, since these men lose height with age, from 176 cm in their late twenties to 172 in their late sixties. Both failures in this segment were read off a picture, and the next segment is what to do when eyeballing is not enough.

D. Testing a claim

From one person’s slice membership to a measure of disagreement and a decision

Judge a departure against sampling noise

A sample differs from its population, even when the population obeys our claim. A difference alone cannot settle the question.

We want a rule that detects departures, while rarely rejecting a claim that is true. To build it, we first need to know what that claim predicts.

Delivery mode only. Begin with the task, rather than a formula or a test name. We need a decision using one noisy sample. A useful rule must respond to departures without treating every random fluctuation as one. These two requirements will eventually give us a measure of disagreement and a false-alarm budget. Neither requires starting with chi-squared.

A precise claim gives us a world to reason under

Suppose independently sampled heights come from a bell with centre 175 cm and SD 7 cm.

\(H_0\): the null hypothesis

The population follows this specified bell.

\(H_1\): the alternative hypothesis

The population does not follow this bell.

Assume \(H_0\) temporarily. What would repeated samples look like if it were true? That assumption lets us calculate sampling noise.

Delivery mode only. Assuming the null for a calculation is not accepting it as fact. It specifies the reference world in which we can calculate probabilities. We are checking the whole distribution, rather than just its centre. Independent sampling is a working assumption from segment C, not something this count test can verify. Fix the claim and test design before using the sample to make the decision.

Population parameters stay fixed; sample statistics change

Quantity Population parameter Sample statistic
Centre \(\mu\) = 175 cm \(\bar y\) = 175.3 cm
Width \(\sigma\) = 7 cm \(s\) = 6.9 cm

The parameters in \(H_0\) stay fixed. A new sample gives new statistics.

A hat marks an estimate: \(\hat\sigma\) estimates the population’s \(\sigma\). Here we check the claimed bell rather than fitting a new one to the sample.

Delivery mode only. The distinction is the job of the number, not its alphabet: sigma-hat is a statistic even though it uses Greek. A mean alone could miss a wrong width or shape. To compare the whole distribution, we will choose a collection of counts that the null predicts. Other tests could make other choices; binning is our design choice for this example.

The bell predicts probabilities for slices

Use the claimed bell’s quantiles to make 10 slices. Under \(H_0\), a person lands in each slice with probability \(\pi=1/k=0.1\).

The probabilities are equal; the slice widths are not.

Delivery mode only. Pi is the probability of one slice, not the p-value. Ten is a convenient choice, not a law of testing. We fix the cuts from the null before inspecting counts. This test can see departures in slice probabilities; a distribution differing only within the slices can escape it. The annotation anticipates the expected count, which we derive next.

For one person, slice membership is a zero or a one

For a fixed slice, write \(I=1\) if the person lands inside it, and \(I=0\) otherwise.

Membership \(I\) Probability under \(H_0\)
\(1\): inside \(\pi\)
\(0\): outside \(1-\pi\)

\[\mathbb E[I]=1\cdot\pi+0\cdot(1-\pi)=\pi\]

\(\mathbb E\) means average over repeated draws. Summing memberships across \(n\) people gives the count \(O=I_1+\cdots+I_n\), so \(\mathbb E[O]=n\pi\).

Delivery mode only. This is a count built from individual observations, not an expected-count formula handed to us. Expectation is a probability weighted average. Add those averages to get n times pi. We do not need independence to add expectations, but we do need it for the variance step. For these men the expectation is 799 times 0.1, or 79.9; a repeated-sample average can be fractional even though a particular count is an integer.

The same zero-or-one variable gives its variance

Because \(I\) is either zero or one, \(I^2=I\), so \(\mathbb E[I^2]=\pi\) too.

\[\operatorname{Var}(I)=\mathbb E[I^2]-\mathbb E[I]^2 =\pi-\pi^2=\pi(1-\pi)\]

Variances add across independent people. Therefore

\[E=n\pi,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(O)=n\pi(1-\pi)=E(1-\pi)\]

Here \(E=79.9\) and \(\operatorname{SD}(O)=\sqrt{79.9\times0.9}\approx8.5\) people. That is the count’s ordinary sampling noise under \(H_0\).

Delivery mode only. The variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Expanding E[(I-E[I])^2] gives E[I^2]-E[I]^2, so the identity does not need a new distribution. Independence is across people. The counts of different slices are not independent, because each person enters just one. A single count has a binomial law, whose variance we have just derived. In particular its variance is E times (1-pi), not E. We will account for the linked counts when constructing the pooled score.

Judge a count gap against its sampling noise

A gap of about ten people is almost five SDs for 50 people, but only about one SD for 799. Raw gaps are not on a common noise scale.

For one slice, measure disagreement in SDs: \(\displaystyle\frac{O-E}{\sqrt{E(1-\pi)}}\).

Delivery mode only. Both pictures are exact binomial probabilities on the same count-gap axis, not normal approximations. The marked gap is an illustration, not a measured integer count. The earlier SD described how heights vary among people; this SD describes how a count varies among surveys. Dividing a gap by its sampling SD standardizes it. The next problem is how to combine the ten slice checks without pretending their counts are independent. Holding a raw gap of ten people fixed is different from holding a population-share departure fixed. A fixed share departure produces a gap proportional to n, while its noise grows like root n; its standardized gap therefore grows like root n. That is why more data can increase power.

The ten gaps are linked by the fixed total

Write \(d_i=O_i-E\). Both sets of counts total \(n\), so

\[d_1+\cdots+d_k=0\]

Only nine gaps can vary freely. Simply adding all ten always gives zero, even when the claimed shape is wrong.

Delivery mode only. One person’s extra membership in one slice uses up a membership that cannot go to another slice. The sum constraint is exact. It rules out treating ten separate standardized gaps as ten independent standard normal variables. Nor does choosing any nine counts make them independent. We need comparisons that separate the nine free directions of variation, which is what the next slides construct.

Compare two slices to isolate one fluctuation

Score each person by whether they enter slice 1 or slice 2.

Person lands in Score \(J\) Probability
Slice 1 \(+1\) \(\pi\)
Slice 2 \(-1\) \(\pi\)
Any other slice \(0\) \(1-2\pi\)

\[\mathbb E[J]=\pi-\pi=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(J)=\pi+\pi=2\pi\]

Summing scores gives \(O_1-O_2\). Independent people give \(\operatorname{Var}(O_1-O_2)=2n\pi=2E\).

Delivery mode only. The score has mean zero, so its variance is simply its average square. The two nonzero outcomes both square to one. We have derived the variance of a difference directly, without assuming that O1 and O2 are independent. Dividing that difference by square root of two gives a comparison of unit length, with variance E. This will explain the denominator in Pearson’s score rather than quietly replacing the single-bin variance E(1-pi) with E.

A balanced comparison has a natural noise scale

Normalize the two-slice difference:

\[C_1=\frac{O_1-O_2}{\sqrt2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbb E[C_1]=0,\quad \operatorname{Var}(C_1)=E\]

Now express it in its own SDs:

\[Z_1=\frac{C_1}{\sqrt E} =\frac{O_1-O_2}{\sqrt{2E}},\qquad \mathbb E[Z_1]=0,\quad\operatorname{Var}(Z_1)=1\]

This is where \(\sqrt E\) comes from: the noise scale of a normalized, balanced comparison of counts.

Delivery mode only. Dividing by square root of two gives coefficients plus one over root two and minus one over root two. Their squared weights sum to one, so this is a direction of unit length. More generally let a person’s score be a_i in slice i, with sum a_i=0 and sum a_i^2=1. Then E[score]=pi sum a_i=0, Var(score)=pi sum a_i^2=pi, and the summed score C has variance n pi=E. Every such balanced comparison has the same noise scale. The statement is exact for our equal-probability slices.

Nine distinct comparisons capture all ten gaps

The first comparison is slice 1 against slice 2. The next compares those two together against slice 3:

\[C_1=\frac{d_1-d_2}{\sqrt2},\qquad C_2=\frac{d_1+d_2-2d_3}{\sqrt6}\]

Continue up to slice 10. Each comparison has mean zero and variance \(E\). Their nine perpendicular directions capture every gap.

\[d_1^2+\cdots+d_{10}^2=C_1^2+\cdots+C_9^2\]

This is Pythagoras: the total squared distance is unchanged when we change perpendicular axes.

Delivery mode only. C2 measures the first pair against the third slice; its weights are (1,1,-2) divided by root six. Its weights sum to zero and their squares sum to one. They are perpendicular to C1’s weights because their dot product is zero. In general C_j=(d_1+…+d_j-j d_{j+1})/sqrt(j(j+1)). The nine unit directions are mutually perpendicular and span the space where sum d_i=0. The remaining, all-counts-together direction has no gap because the total is fixed. Pythagoras therefore gives the displayed identity. For perpendicular weight vectors a and b, the single-person score covariance is pi sum a_i b_i=0. Thus the C_j are uncorrelated exactly; we will only claim independence in their joint normal approximation.

Square the standardized comparisons and add

Use a squared distance so both signs of departure contribute. Each comparison has \(Z_j=C_j/\sqrt E\).

\[Q=\sum_{j=1}^{9}Z_j^2 =\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{9}C_j^2}{E} =\sum_{i=1}^{10}\frac{(O_i-E)^2}{E}\]

This is Pearson’s test statistic. The denominator comes from the comparisons’ variance; the sum comes from pooling their squared distances. For these men, \(Q_{\text{obs}} =\) 3.6.

Since each \(Z_j\) has mean zero and variance one, \(\mathbb E[Q\mid H_0]=9\), even before any normal approximation.

Delivery mode only. Squares are a deliberate choice, not the only way to measure departure: absolute distances and likelihood-based scores also lead to tests. Squared distance is useful here because it preserves the distance when we change to perpendicular comparisons, and connects to a simple reference distribution. We derived this for equal probabilities, so E is common. The general Pearson formula uses E_i=n pi_i and needs a corresponding weighted construction. Do not call the ten bin contributions ten independent Z-squares: they are ten terms representing nine directions. As a numerical check, the 71-person slice contributes (71-79.9)^2/79.9 about 0.99; adding all ten gives 3.59. The exact null mean follows from E[Z_j^2]=Var(Z_j)+E[Z_j]^2=1, not from fitted simulation output.

Squared normal comparisons give a chi-squared law

Each comparison sums many independent person scores. With large expected counts, the perpendicular comparisons are approximately normal and independent.

\[Z_1,\ldots,Z_9\ \approx\ \text{independent }N(0,1)\]

A chi-squared distribution is the distribution of a sum of squared, independent standard normal variables. Therefore

\[Q=Z_1^2+\cdots+Z_9^2\ \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\ \chi^2_9\]

Nine degrees of freedom: nine free comparisons, rather than ten independent counts.

Delivery mode only. This step uses the multivariate central limit theorem; normality is a large-sample approximation, not an exact property of counts. The joint normal limit has zero correlations between the perpendicular comparisons, making its components independent. Zero covariance alone would not give independence for the finite-sample counts. The definition of chi-squared is the sum of independent standard normal squares, so its appearance now follows from the construction. All expected counts here are 79.9, and no parameters have been estimated from the sample. The approximation is conditional on the independent-sampling null model. Reference: https://www.math.csi.cuny.edu/~mvj/MTH410/lectures/L21.html

Simulation checks the reference we have derived

Generate 799 independent heights under \(H_0\), count their slices, and compute \(Q\). Repeat 5,000 times.

The null distribution is the spread of \(Q\) when \(H_0\) is true. Its simulated mean is 9.0, against the derived mean of 9.

Delivery mode only. These are invented samples under the claim, not resamples of the observed men. The simulation checks the normal-limit approximation and can supply a reference even when no named curve is available. Exercise 0.5 rebuilds this exact 5,000-survey, seed-0 simulation. We have a statistic and its null distribution. Next we need a decision rule and a budget for false alarms; the statistic alone cannot decide.

A decision rule needs a boundary

Choose a boundary \(c\), the critical value. It separates two possible decisions:

\(Q<c\): fail to reject \(H_0\)

The disagreement is not large enough to reject the claim.

\(Q\ge c\): reject \(H_0\)

The disagreement is large enough to count as evidence against the claim.

\[R=\{Q:Q\ge c\}\qquad\text{the rejection region}\]

Large \(Q\) contradicts this claim, so \(R\) is in the right tail. How should we choose \(c\)?

Delivery mode only. The direction comes from our statistic: shortages and excesses both increase Q after squaring. A two-sided question about heights therefore leads to a right-tailed test of Q. Another statistic might need a left tail or both tails. We have defined the rule’s form, but still owe the room a reason for putting the boundary in one place rather than another.

Choose the false-alarm rate before the decision

Even when \(H_0\) is true, a random sample can land in \(R\). Rejecting a true null is a Type I error, or false alarm.

\[\alpha=P(Q\in R\mid H_0)=P(Q\ge c\mid H_0)\]

Choose \(\alpha=0.05\): a rule designed to reject about 5 in 100 samples when \(H_0\) is true. This chosen risk is the significance level.

Read \(\mid H_0\) as assuming the claim is true. This is a rate across repeated samples, not the probability that our current decision is wrong.

Delivery mode only. Choose the claim, test, and significance level before examining the result. Five percent is a design choice, not a law. A smaller alpha requires stronger disagreement and reduces false alarms, but makes real departures harder to detect. We say about five because we are using a continuous chi-squared approximation to a finite-sample count statistic. The conditioning bar restricts the probability to repeated surveys under H0.

The 95th percentile leaves 5% for false alarms

\[c=F^{-1}_{\chi^2_9}(0.95)\approx16.9\]

Our rule: reject \(H_0\) if \(Q\ge\) 16.9. In the simulated surveys, 4.9% cross this boundary.

Delivery mode only. This is the quantile idea from segment B applied to Q rather than to a height. F inverse denotes the quantile function: the cut leaving 95 percent below and 5 percent above. The shaded area is alpha; the location of the cut is c. Those are different quantities. The simulation is a check on the chi-squared approximation, not a reason to adjust alpha after seeing the men. We can finally apply the complete rule to the data.

The men’s statistic does not cross the boundary

\(Q_{\text{obs}} =\) 3.6 \(<\) 16.9. Fail to reject \(H_0\) at the 5% significance level. These data do not provide enough evidence against the specified bell.

Failing to reject does not prove the claim. A false claim can also produce a sample that stays below the boundary.

Delivery mode only. Keep the conclusion limited to this test and this sample. A large range of nearby population distributions could give a non-rejection too. We therefore do not call the left side an acceptance region. Reject and fail to reject describe evidence, not knowledge of which population generated the sample. We will name the missed-detection error in segment E.

The same rule rejects the pooled sample

The shortest slice expects 163 people and contains 684. Now \(Q =\) 1,906 \(>\) 16.9: reject \(H_0\).

Adding the women changes the population mix. The same calculation detects the distribution mismatch that segment C showed in pictures.

Delivery mode only. With the new sample size, each expected count becomes 1,629 divided by ten. The claim and its cuts stay fixed, and the chi-squared reference still has nine degrees of freedom. The test pools discrepancies across the slices; it does not diagnose their cause. Nor does it establish independence: a sample with the right marginal shape but dependent pairs may not reveal that dependence through these counts.

Move the slider through surveys generated under \(H_0\), then select either real sample. The boundary stays fixed; the sample statistic changes.

Delivery mode only. Some surveys generated under H0 still trigger rejection. They are the false alarms we allowed when choosing alpha. The displayed fraction is measured across all 5,000 simulated surveys. Decisions use the unrounded values; one decimal place is only the display format. The pooled sample is beyond the axis, so its marker sits at the right edge and is labelled off scale. E. The p-value

What it is, how to read it off the null, and the misreadings

Replace the fixed boundary with the observed statistic

We used a fixed boundary \(c\) to make a decision. Now ask:

If \(H_0\) were true, how often would \(Q\) be at least as large as \(Q_{\text{obs}}\)?

\[p=P(Q\ge Q_{\text{obs}}\mid H_0)\]

This probability is the p-value. It is a tail area beyond the observed statistic, computed assuming the null hypothesis is true.

Delivery mode only. Alpha was a tail area we chose before seeing the result; p is the tail area determined by the result. The conditioning bar has the same meaning as on the false-alarm slide. Here larger Q is more extreme. With another statistic, more extreme might mean smaller values or distance in either direction; the relevant tail comes from the test’s alternative.

Count the null surveys beyond the observed statistic

About 93% of the 5,000 simulated surveys have \(Q\ge Q_{\text{obs}}\). That share estimates the p-value. The chi-squared approximation gives \(p =\) 0.94.

Delivery mode only. We move the start of the shaded tail from c to Q_obs. The simulation estimates this area by counting; the chi-squared curve approximates it analytically. Neither is an exact finite-sample answer. This is a large tail: a Q at least as large as the men’s is common under the null. It supplies little evidence against the claim, but does not assign the claim a probability of being true.

The p-value gives the same decision rule

Compare the statistic

\[Q_{\text{obs}}\ge c\]

The observed disagreement reaches the rejection region.

Compare the tail area

\[p\le\alpha\]

The tail beyond the observation is no larger than the allowed false-alarm area.

\[\text{Reject }H_0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\le\alpha\]

For the men, \(p =\) 0.94 \(>0.05\): fail to reject \(H_0\), just as the statistic rule did.

Delivery mode only. Both comparisons use the same continuous chi-squared reference. Moving Q farther right shrinks its tail. At c the tail is alpha; beyond c it is smaller. This is why a small p is evidence against H0, and why alpha must be selected before inspecting p. Do not reverse the inequality: rejection needs p below alpha, not above it.

p is not the probability that H0 is true

What \(p\) measures

\[P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)\]

A share of the 5,000 null surveys, every one of which obeys the claim.

What people hear

\[P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\]

A verdict on the claim itself. Different question, and answering it needs a prior.

\(p\) also does not tell us the probability that chance caused the result. It measures how extreme the statistic is under an assumed \(H_0\).

Delivery mode only. Reversing the conditioning asks a different question. The probability that the claim is true given the data is a Bayesian question requiring a prior and a model for alternatives. Calling p the probability that chance caused the result makes the same unsupported leap from a probability under H0 to a verdict on what produced this sample.

A small p means detectable, not large

The claimed centre is 173.5 cm, 1.8 cm below the full sample’s mean. Compare all 799 men with the first 50.

All 799: \(p =\) 6e-09; first 50: \(p =\) 0.57. Judge practical size separately.

Delivery mode only. Both panels lean the same way, too few short men and too many tall ones, because it is one set of men measured against one wrong claim. They are nested samples, not identical measured effects: the first 50 have their own sampling variation. For fixed observed proportions, Q grows with n, so a modest departure can become detectable with more data. The smaller sample has expected counts of five, making its chi-squared approximation rougher too. Day 2’s strongest benchmark has the smallest p: detectability alone cannot rank practical quality or effect size.

A test can make two kinds of error

Our decision \(H_0\) is true A specific alternative is true
Reject \(H_0\) Type I error: false alarm Correct detection
Fail to reject \(H_0\) Correct non-rejection Type II error: missed detection

Power is the probability of rejecting \(H_0\) when a particular alternative is true: \(1-\beta\), where \(\beta\) is its missed-detection rate.

More data or a larger departure usually increases power. For a fixed test and sample size, lowering \(\alpha\) reduces false alarms but also reduces power.

Delivery mode only. Alpha describes the decision rule when the null is true. Beta and power depend on a particular alternative: a bell shifted by a tenth of a centimetre is harder to detect than one shifted by ten. There is no single power for every possible departure. The larger-sample comparison on the previous slide illustrates detectability, not a power estimate; estimating power requires repeated samples under a specified alternative. Failing to reject can therefore mean either a true null or an undetected departure, which is why we do not say accept H0.

A tiny p means unfinished

The pooled survey’s \(Q\) is 1,906, so far into the tail that its \(p\) underflows to zero. That does not make the claim worthless: it means one bell cannot describe two populations, and the test can tell.

Every method in this course leaves something behind, and a test is how you find out whether it is more than luck. A tiny \(p\) means unfinished.

Delivery mode only. The payoff of the deck. Both halves matter: unfinished, because something is left unaccounted for; not worthless, because the same claim was not rejected for the men earlier in the deck. On Day 2 the p is about 1e-240 for the hardest benchmark in the course to beat, and that pair of facts is the opening for Day 3.
