A. Randomness has a shape

The random variable, the distribution, and the two numbers that summarize it

Before it is measured, a value is a random variable

Capital \(Y\) marks a value not yet known; lowercase \(y\) is what it turns out to be.

Symbol Meaning
\(Y\) the height of the next person you measure: still random
\(y\) the same height, once measured: a number

A random variable is the whole set of values \(Y\) could take, before it is seen. Statistics is the study of that set.

Delivery mode only. The capital and lowercase distinction is the one that trips people later, so give it a sentence. The next slide names the sample every picture in this deck is drawn on.

Every picture here is drawn on the same 799 heights

The 799 men of a national health survey, each one measured standing against a board rather than asked. Before it is anything else it is a pile of 799 numbers, and every idea here is a question asked of them.

NHANES I Epidemiologic Follow-up Study · where these numbers come from

Delivery mode only. The survey is the NHANES follow-up study, and the heights are measured to a fraction of a centimetre. That matters more than it sounds: a sample rounded to the inch piles onto a lattice, and segment D’s test then rejects it on the rounding alone. One sample, five questions: what shape, cut where, one population or two, and what a test of the claim says.

A distribution is the shape of the whole set of values

A distribution is the full set of plausible values and how often each occurs. Its picture is a histogram; the mean is one number inside it.

Delivery mode only. Same numbers as the previous slide, the pile tipped into bins. The histogram is the distribution made visible. The next slide puts two numbers on it.

The mean is the centre, the SD is the width

\[\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i\]

\[s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}\]

\(\bar{y}\) is 175.3 cm, the centre of the pile; \(s\) is 6.9 cm, the typical distance from it. \(s^2\) is the variance: that width squared, and the form that adds up on Day 1.

Delivery mode only. Read the picture before the formulas: the SD is a width, and the shaded bands and the shares they cover are what makes it one. Then the formulas, left to right in words, because the course writes both, and the mean before the SD because the SD is built out of it. The bar means averaged and the hat, two segments from now, means estimated. If anyone asks why the SD divides by n-1 rather than n: the deviations are measured from the sample’s own mean rather than the true one, so they come out slightly too small, and the smaller divisor puts that back. Worth saying that a tall person is not an error, they are the tail of the shape. Say the variance out loud even though nothing here uses it: on Day 1 independent errors add variances rather than SDs, and that is where the square comes back. The next slide is the curve those two numbers pin exactly.

The bell curve is those two numbers and nothing else

\[Y \sim N(\mu,\ \sigma^2)\]

Read \(\sim\) as is distributed as: \(\mu\) the centre, \(\sigma\) the width.

  • A bell puts 68% of its values inside one SD, and 95% inside two
  • These men put 68% and 96% there: close, and measured

Two numbers, one whole shape. Those 68% and 95% are shares of it, and a share of a shape has a name of its own.

Delivery mode only. The gap between the histogram and the curve is the price of the assumption, and it is visible here rather than asserted. Segment D comes back and tests this claim properly. The one line of notation is the line every model in the course is written in, so read it out: Y is distributed as a normal with centre mu and width sigma. Greek because the pair describes the bell rather than any sample, which is the distinction segment D sharpens. The squared sigma inside the bracket is the variance from the last slide, and the bracket is where the course always puts it. The next slide names the shares it just quoted.

A probability is a share of the whole pile

\(P(\text{something})\) is the share of the pile where that something is true, a number between 0 and 1.

Notation In words Here
\(P(Y \le 180)\) the next man measures 180 cm or less 0.76
\(P(Y > 180)\) he measures more 0.24
\(P(\lvert Y - \bar{y} \rvert \le s)\) he lands within one SD of the centre 0.68

Capital \(Y\) is doing the work it was introduced for: a probability is a question about the value before it is measured. What people usually quote about a height is the other direction, a cut.

Delivery mode only. Nothing here is new arithmetic, it is a name and a bracket for shares the last two slides already read off the picture: the third row is that same 68%, counted off these men. Say that the two rows either side of 180 cm sum to 1, because a probability is a share of one whole pile. This slide exists because segment E argues about this notation, and the misreadings there are about what is inside the bracket. The next segment stops summarizing the shape and starts cutting it.

B. Cutting a distribution

The median, the quantile, the percentile, and what an 80% range is

The median is the cut that leaves half on each side

The median is a position in a sorted list, not a formula. Sort the 799 heights; the median is number 400, at 175 cm, with half the people below and half above.

Delivery mode only. Start from the median because the room already owns it. Left panel is the sorted list, right panel the same numbers as a histogram. The next slide changes one number and the median becomes a general quantile.

A quantile is the same cut, with a knob

  • The \(0.5\) quantile is the median: position 400 of 799
  • The \(0.9\) quantile is position 719, the height 184 cm
  • The \(0.1\) quantile is position 80, the height 167 cm

One rule, one number. A quantile is a position in a sorted list, and the number on the front is the knob.

Delivery mode only. Same picture as the previous slide with the pair of cuts added, so the knob is the only thing that changed. Say “half the list”, not “half the range”: on a skewed sample the midpoint of the range is nowhere near the median.

An 80% range is a pair of cuts

An 80% range leaves 10% of the people in each tail, so its two ends are the \(0.1\) and \(0.9\) quantiles: 167 cm and 184 cm here.

A 95% range leaves 2.5% in each tail, so its ends are the \(0.025\) and \(0.975\) quantiles, 161 cm and 188 cm. Same rule, different knob.

Every interval the course later puts around a forecast is a pair of cuts like these. Nothing else changes between an 80% one and a 95% one.

Delivery mode only. This is the slide deck 3’s interval segment and deck 4’s CRPS both assume. Worth saying out loud that the two cuts need not be symmetric around the mean, and on a skewed sample they are not.

A percentile is the same cut, read as a share

The 90th percentile is the height below which 90% of the people fall. The \(0.9\) quantile and the 90th percentile are the same number, 184 cm.

A quantile is a position in one sorted list. It says nothing about where the next value comes from.

A distribution tells you the shape of one sample. It does not tell you whether the next value is drawn from that same shape, or whether the sample was collected in a way that lets you ask.

Delivery mode only. Quantile and percentile are one object with two names, so do not spend time on the distinction. The second half of the slide is the gap: every method in the course that reuses past values assumes the next one is drawn from the same distribution, and that assumption has a name.

C. i.i.d.

Independent, then identically distributed, then each one breaking on this survey

i.i.d. is two claims, and they fail differently

Independent

Each value tells you nothing about the next one.

Identically distributed

Every value in the sample comes from the same distribution.

They are separate claims, and each breaks its own way. One careless survey breaks the first. A different careless survey breaks the second.

Delivery mode only. The two claims are the whole segment. The next two slides take independence apart; the two after that take identical distribution apart. Do not conflate them: the deck’s whole i.i.d. story is that a sample can satisfy one and fail the other.

Independent means one says nothing about the other

Take 605 pairs of men from different families and plot each pair. If the two are independent the cloud has no tilt, and the correlation sits at -0.02 on a scale running from \(-1\) to \(+1\).

Now pair each man with his own brother instead. What happens to the cloud?

Delivery mode only. These are real men out of Galton’s 1886 study, paired at random across families. The scatter is the definition made visible, and the clean case is seen before the broken one. The next slide keeps the same men and the same number of pairs, and changes only who is paired with whom.

Pair each man with his own brother instead

  • The same 605 pairs, now correlating at +0.39
  • Read one brother’s height and you can guess the other’s

Independence fails when one value carries information about another. Same men, same histogram, different pairing.

Galton (1886), 934 children in 205 families · where these numbers come from

Delivery mode only. The tilt is the failure, and the last sentence is the part that matters: a broken independence claim is invisible in a histogram, which is why it needs its own picture. This is Galton’s own finding, and the deck is using it for the reason he collected it. On Day 1 the pair stops being two brothers and becomes this month and last month. The next two slides take the second half of i.i.d. apart.

Identically distributed means one shape throughout

Split the men in two and look at each half. If both sit at the same level with the same spread, one distribution stands for all of them, and the histogram has one hump.

Now split the survey by something that is not arbitrary.

Delivery mode only. Two panels, one claim: the boxes are level, so a single mean and a single SD are honest for the whole sample. The split is arbitrary on purpose, because any split of one population gives this picture. The next slide is the same figure on a sample that holds two.

One survey, two populations, and one bell for both

The same survey also measured 830 women. Two overlapping populations, and a pooled SD of 9.1 cm against 6.9 and 5.8 inside the groups.

Identical distribution fails when one sample holds two populations. Its pooled 80% range, 157 to 181 cm, is right for nobody.

Delivery mode only. Nothing is wrong with either group; what is wrong is one bell standing for both. This is the failure the course pays for later: on Day 2 a band built from every error a model ever made is meant to cover 80% and covers 61%, for exactly this reason. A subtler real version sits in the same file, since these men lose height with age, from 176 cm in their late twenties to 172 in their late sixties. Both failures in this segment were read off a picture, and the next segment is what to do when eyeballing is not enough.

D. Testing a claim

H0 and H1, the test statistic, the null distribution, alpha, the decision

A claim worth checking, before we check it

Suppose these men come from a bell centred on 175 cm with a standard deviation of 7 cm.

That 175 is a parameter, a fact about every man alive that nobody will ever measure. The 175.3 cm these 799 came out at is a statistic.

Eyeballing one against the other cannot say whether the gap between them is real or luck. The rest of this segment is how you ask properly.

Delivery mode only. The course moment this unlocks: 24 lag checks by eye become one number (deck 3, segment B). Here it is ten slices instead of 24 lags, and the machinery is the same. Say out loud that the two numbers are stated before the data is looked at rather than fitted to it, because that is what keeps the degrees of freedom at nine three slides from now. The next slide is the alphabet that distinction is written in.

Greek is what you cannot measure, Latin is what you counted

parameter, in Greek statistic, in Latin
centre \(\mu\) = 175 cm, claimed \(\bar{y}\) = 175.3 cm, measured
width \(\sigma\) = 7 cm, claimed \(s\) = 6.9 cm, measured

A hat marks a parameter that was estimated from data rather than known: \(\hat{\sigma}\) is a guess at \(\sigma\), so it is a statistic like any other. Day 1 puts a hat on almost everything.

Delivery mode only. The alphabet rule the whole course runs on, and it takes fifteen seconds: Greek for what you can never measure, Latin for what you counted. The hat is the part that pays off soonest, because on Day 1 sigma-hat is the width of the residuals and every interval in the course is built from it. The next slide turns the claim into something countable.

The claim is a prediction you can count

Cut the claimed bell into 10 slices that each hold the same share. The cuts are quantiles, from the last segment, and the claim now predicts a count: about 80 men per slice.

Delivery mode only. Equal shares are the whole trick: every slice expects the same count, so the reader compares ten numbers against one number instead of against a table. The outer slices are wide and short because what is equal between slices is area, not width. The next slide counts the sample into them.

Every slice is a separate check the claim has to pass

If the claim is right, each slice should hold about 80 of the 799 men. The orange blocks are the gaps, and no single one of them looks alarming.

Ten separate checks, and with ten chances one gap looks large by luck alone reasonably often. Judging them one at a time is how you talk yourself into anything.

Delivery mode only. The multiple-comparisons point is why a test exists at all, so do not skip it. Ten bars on the screen is what makes pooling them into one number feel necessary rather than arbitrary. The next slide names the claim formally.

Name the claim, and then its opposite

\(H_0\): the null

The men come from the claimed bell, so every slice holds about 80 of them.

\(H_1\): the alternative

They do not. At least one slice is off by more than luck explains.

The null is the boring version of the world, because that is the one a single number can be measured against.

Delivery mode only. Note the asymmetry as you read the columns: the test can reject the null and can fail to reject it, but it cannot confirm it, which is the fence at the end of segment E. The next slide builds the number the two columns get decided by.

The ten gaps pool into one number

one slice’s gap

\[O_i - E_i\]

short and long both count

\[(O_i - E_i)^2\]

scaled by what was expected

\[\frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\]

\[Q = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\]

\(Q\) is the orange blocks from the last slide, squared, scaled and added over all 10 slices. Ten checks in, one number out. If the claim is true, what should it come out at?

Delivery mode only. Build it one step at a time and read each step out: the gap in a slice, squared so a short slice and a long slice both add to it, then divided by E so a gap of ten counts for less where more people were expected. O is the men counted in slice i, E is the 80 the claim predicts, k is the 10 slices. Nothing new arrives here, it is the orange blocks from the last slide with the arithmetic named. Worth saying that Q grows with the number of people measured, because segment E’s last slide is built on exactly that. Take the closing question seriously before advancing: most rooms answer zero.

Simulate the claim being true and Q still wobbles

Invent 20,000 surveys of 799 men drawn from the claimed bell, so the claim is true by construction. Every one of them still lands its slices a little off, so every one of them still has a \(Q\).

\(Q\) is never zero, even when nothing is wrong. It averages 9.0. What the men’s \(Q\) has to be compared against is this whole pile, not zero.

Delivery mode only. The key idea, and the one people skip: under a true null Q is still a random variable. This is the slide that earns the next one, so do not rush it. Nothing about the real men has been used yet; these are invented surveys of an invented population that obeys the claim exactly. The next slide puts a name on the pile.

That wobble is the chi-squared distribution

  • Its one setting is the degrees of freedom, 9 here rather than 10
  • Nine slice counts and the total fix the tenth, so only 9 can move

This is the null distribution of \(Q\), and it is a sampling distribution: the spread a number picks up purely from being computed on a sample. Knowing it is what turns one number into a decision.

Delivery mode only. The curve is not a new claim, it is a name for the histogram already on the screen, and it lies on it because the claim was stated in advance rather than fitted to the men. Fitting the centre and the width instead spends two more degrees of freedom: simulated the same way, that pile averages 7.4 rather than 9.0, and the deck would owe the room a correction. Say “sampling distribution” out loud, because the picture returns twice: on Day 1 as the plus-or-minus 1.96 over root T band on an ACF, and on Day 2 as the standard error of a coverage rate. Same idea, different statistic each time. The next slide cuts this shape at 5%.

The critical value is where the top 5% begins

Alpha is the false-alarm rate you accept: \(\alpha = 0.05\) means accepting a 5% chance of rejecting the claim when it is true. It is fixed before the data is looked at.

Of those invented surveys, 5.1% landed past 16.9: alpha, counted.

The critical value is the 95th percentile of the null: a quantile, cut from segment B. Reject \(H_0\) if \(Q\) lands past 16.9.

Delivery mode only. Alpha is a choice, not a property of the data, and 0.05 is this course’s. Name the link back to segment B out loud: the critical value is a quantile of the null distribution, the same cut in a different sorted list. If anyone has read ahead, head off the collision: deck 4 reuses the letter alpha for a quantile level rather than a false-alarm rate, so its alpha runs over the whole ladder from 0.025 to 0.975 and means the cut itself. Same letter, unrelated job. The next slide is the verdict on the honest survey.

The men’s Q lands inside the wobble

\(Q\) is 3.6 against a critical value of 16.9. These men are indistinguishable from the claim, which is not the same as showing the claim is true.

Delivery mode only. Same picture as the slide before it, so the observed line drops onto a chart the room is already reading. Say “indistinguishable from”, never “accept H0”: this sample is also indistinguishable from a claim of 175.4, and both cannot be true. Worth naming that this is real measured data passing a real test, not a sample built to pass one. The next slide runs the same test on the same survey with its women added back in.

Add the women back and Q lands far out in the tail

  • The shortest slice expected 163 people and got 684
  • \(Q\) is 1,906, so the claim is rejected

The same test, the same claim, one sample holding two populations. One number now says what segment C needed two pictures to show.

Delivery mode only. The verdict, and the payoff of the segment: the test caught the failure segment C had to eyeball. Worth noting what it cannot catch: this test looks only at the shape, so a sample with the right shape and the wrong pairs, which is Galton’s brothers two segments ago, sails through it. A test answers one question. The next segment takes apart the number the verdict was read off.

E. The p-value

What it is, how to read it off the null, and the misreadings

The p-value is how often this extreme happens under H0

\[p = P\big(Q \ge Q_{\text{obs}}\big)\]

\[p = P\big(Q \ge Q_{\text{obs}} \;\big|\; H_0\big)\]

How often a statistic at least this extreme turns up, when the claim is true.

Read the bar as given: \(P(A \mid B)\) is how often \(A\) happens among the cases where \(B\) holds, which is not how often \(A\) happens.

\(p\) conditions on \(H_0\). It measures how surprising the data would be if the claim were true, and nothing else.

Delivery mode only. The first line is what most people carry around, and it is struck out for a reason: the probability of a statistic this extreme is not a quantity anyone can compute without saying under what. Read the second line out in words, left to right, including the bar as “given”. The P is segment A’s, a share of a pile, and the only new mark is the bar, so spend the time there: it restricts which pile is being shared out. Concretely, the pile is the 20,000 invented surveys segment D simulated, every one of which obeys the claim. The conditioning is the whole slide, and the two misreadings later both come from dropping it. The next slide reads p off the picture.

Read it off the null distribution, at the observed value

Or count it instead: 94% of the 20,000 invented surveys produced a \(Q\) at least this big. The area and the count are the same number.

The \(p\)-value is the tail area beyond the observed \(Q\), under the null. Here it is 0.94, so a \(Q\) this big or bigger happens 94% of the time when the claim is true.

Delivery mode only. Same chart as the segment’s critical-value slide, now with the tail as the quantity of interest rather than the threshold. This is also where the alpha of 0.05 and the p-value connect: p past the threshold and Q past the critical value are the same statement. The next slides are the misreadings.

p is not the probability that H0 is true

What \(p\) measures

\[P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)\]

A share of the 20,000 invented surveys, every one of which obeys the claim.

What people hear

\[P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\]

A verdict on the claim itself. Different question, and answering it needs a prior.

Nor is it the probability that the result happened by chance: that would be \(P(\text{data})\), with no bar at all.

Both misreadings drop the conditioning bar. \(p\) is a tail area under an assumed null, not a verdict on the null and not a probability of the data.

Delivery mode only. These are the two most common misreadings and they have the same cause, which is why they share a slide. If the room wants the probability the claim is true, that is a Bayesian question and it needs a prior. The next slide is the third misreading, and it needs a picture.

A small p means detectable, not large

One wrong claim, 1.8 cm below what these men measure, tested on all 799 of them and then on the first 50. Same error both times: how far apart can the two \(p\)-values be?

6e-09 against 0.57. \(p\) answers a question about sample size as much as about the size of the difference, so it ranks nothing. Never sort competing claims by \(p\).

Delivery mode only. Both panels lean the same way, too few short men and too many tall ones, because it is one set of men measured against one wrong claim; only how many were counted changed. This is the T factor in the Q formula coming back. Say the reverse out loud too: on a very large sample, a difference too small to care about will still reject. The no-ranking fence in the card is the one that bites on Day 2, where the best benchmark model in the course has the smallest p of any of them, because p answers a question about detectability rather than quality.

A tiny p means unfinished, not very wrong

The pooled survey’s \(Q\) is 1,906, so far into the tail that its \(p\) underflows to zero. That does not make the claim worthless: it means one bell cannot describe two populations, and the test can tell.

Every method in this course leaves something behind, and a test is how you find out whether it is more than luck. A tiny \(p\) means unfinished.

Delivery mode only. The payoff of the deck. Both halves matter: unfinished, because something is left unaccounted for; not worthless, because the same claim described the men perfectly well two slides ago. On Day 2 the p is about 1e-240 for the hardest benchmark in the course to beat, and that pair of facts is the opening for Day 3. The next slide maps every idea back into the decks.
