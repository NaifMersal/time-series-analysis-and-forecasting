A. The distribution

The range, the random variable, the shape, and the cuts that make a band

A point forecast is one number, and the future is not

A point forecast is a single centre, usually a mean or a median.

The future value has not been observed yet. Until it is, it is still random, and one number cannot represent a range of plausible outcomes.

A forecast without a range is half an answer. The range is the forecast; the point is one summary of it.

Delivery mode only. The course moment this unlocks: a forecast is a range, not a number (deck 3, segment C). The next slide names the object that holds the range.

Before it is observed, the future is a random variable

Capital \(Y\) marks a value not yet observed; lowercase \(y\) is what it turns out to be.

Symbol Meaning
\(Y_{T+h}\) the future value, still random
\(y_{T+h}\) the same value, once observed
\(\hat{y}_{T+h}\) the point forecast, one number

A random variable is the whole set of values \(Y\) could take, before it is seen. The point forecast is one number chosen from that set.

Delivery mode only. The capital/lowercase distinction is the one that trips people later, so give it a sentence. The point forecast is a summary of the random variable, not the random variable itself.

The distribution is the shape of that randomness

A distribution is the full set of plausible values, not one. Its picture is a histogram of many draws.

Delivery mode only. The histogram is the distribution made visible. The point forecast sits somewhere inside it; the band spans most of it. The next slide shows the shape is stable across many simulations.

The same recipe gives 5,000 futures, and they share one shape

Repeated simulation gives a stable shape. That shape is the distribution, and it does not depend on which 8 paths you happened to look at.

Delivery mode only. The left panel is 8 paths; the right is all 5000 cut at the quantiles. The shape is the same in both, which is the point: the distribution is a property of the recipe, not of one draw.

The shape is the forecast, the line is just its centre

The band is the forecast. The line down the middle is just its most likely value.

Delivery mode only. This is the segment’s payoff. The band is the object the course scores; the point is one summary. The next segment asks how to cut the band from the distribution.

B. Cutting a distribution

The quantile, the percentile, and why an 80% interval is a pair of cuts

The median is the cut that leaves half on each side

The median is a position in a sorted list, not a formula. Sort the values; the median is the one with half below and half above.

Delivery mode only. Start from the median because the room already owns it. The next slide changes one number and the median becomes a general quantile.

A quantile is the same cut, with a knob

  • The \(0.5\) quantile is the median: position \(0.5n\)
  • The \(0.9\) quantile is position \(0.9n\): 90% of the values sit below it
  • The \(0.1\) quantile is position \(0.1n\): 10% sit below it

One rule, one number. A quantile is a position in a sorted list, and the number is the knob.

Delivery mode only. The median is the 0.5 quantile; every other quantile is the same sorted list read at a different position. Say “half the list”, not “half the range”, because on skewed data the midpoint of the range is nowhere near the median.

An 80% interval is a pair of cuts

The 80% interval is the span between the \(0.1\) and \(0.9\) quantiles. Two cuts, one band.

Delivery mode only. The 80% interval leaves 10% in each tail, so its bounds are the 0.1 and 0.9 quantiles. The 95% interval leaves 2.5% in each tail, so its bounds are the 0.025 and 0.975 quantiles. Same rule, different knob.

CRPS integrates a ladder of cuts

CRPS scores the whole distribution by integrating the quantile loss over a ladder of cuts. Each cut is priced by its own slope.

Delivery mode only. The course moment this unlocks: CRPS integrates a ladder of quantiles. The pinball loss is the price of one cut; CRPS is the integral of that price over the whole ladder. The next slide names the percentile, which is the same cut read as a share.

A percentile is the same cut, read as a share

The 90th percentile is the value below which 90% of the data fall.

A quantile is a position in a sorted list. A percentile is the same position, read as a share of the data. The \(0.9\) quantile and the 90th percentile are the same number.

Quantile: a position. Percentile: the same position, read as a share. One object, two names.

Delivery mode only. The distinction is naming, not substance. The course uses “quantile” for the position and “percentile” for the share; they are the same cut. The next slide names the gap this segment opens.

Quantiles describe one distribution

A quantile is a position in one sorted list. It says nothing about where the next value comes from.

The distribution tells you the shape of one draw. It does not tell you whether the next draw comes from the same shape.

Delivery mode only. This is the gap the segment opens. A quantile is a property of one distribution; it says nothing about whether the future error comes from the same distribution as the past ones. That is the i.i.d. assumption, and it is the next segment.

To build a band from past errors, they must be exchangeable

The bootstrap and conformal both assume the past errors stand in for the future ones.

Past errorsthe pool Draw from the poolwith replacement Simulate forwardone path at a time Cut the quantilesthe band

The assumption doing the work: the past errors are exchangeable with the future ones. That is i.i.d., and it is the next segment.

Delivery mode only. The flowrow is the bootstrap in four steps. The assumption is exchangeability: shuffle the past errors and the future error is still comparable. That is i.i.d., and the next segment takes it apart.

C. i.i.d.

Independent, then identically distributed, then each one breaking on the spine

i.i.d. is two claims, and they fail differently

Independent

The last error tells you nothing about the next.

Identically distributed

One spread for every era, 1982 to 2018.

They are separate claims, and each breaks its own way. Independence fails through autocorrelation. Identical distribution fails through a changing variance regime.

Delivery mode only. The two claims are the whole segment. The next two slides take independence apart; the two after that take identical distribution apart. Do not conflate them; the 61% story lives in the second.

Independent means the last one tells you nothing

If \(e_t\) and \(e_{t-1}\) are independent, the scatter of one against the other has no shape. Knowing the last error says nothing about the next.

Delivery mode only. The scatter is the definition made visible. White noise has no shape, which is what independence looks like. The next slide shows the naive’s residuals do have structure, which is the failure.

The naive’s errors still know what month it is

  • Lags 12 and 24 (orange) stand at 0.68 and 0.58, against a bound of 0.096
  • The errors are not independent: the season is still in them

Independence fails through autocorrelation. The naive’s residuals still know what month it is.

Delivery mode only. The orange spikes at 12 and 24 are the failure. The seasonal pattern went through the model and came out untouched. This is the first half of i.i.d. breaking. The next two slides take the second half apart.

Identically distributed means one spread for every era

If the spread is the same in 1985 and 2015, one pool stands for both. That is identically distributed: one \(\sigma\) for the whole series.

Delivery mode only. The rolling SD is the definition made visible. If it is flat, the spread is constant and one pool is honest. The next slide shows it is not flat on this series.

The spread tripled, so the pool mixes two series

  • The residual SD went 8.5 to 26.2: 3x as tall
  • A pooled pool is too tight for recent data: it draws 1980s-sized errors into a 2019 forecast

Identical distribution fails through a changing variance regime. The pool mixes two series.

Delivery mode only. The two boxes are the finding. The left is the 1980s spread; the right is the 2010s spread, three times taller. A pool mixing the two is too tight for recent data. The next slide shows what that does to coverage.

Pool 1982 with 2018 and coverage falls to 61%

  • Full pool: the 80% band covers 61% across 8 folds
  • Recent pool: the same band covers 89%
  • A pool that mixes two spreads is too tight for the recent one

The pooled pool under-covers because it is too tight. It draws 1980s-sized errors into a 2019 forecast.

Delivery mode only. The 61% is the i.i.d. assumption being punished, measured the way Day 2 measures it: 8 rolling folds. The next slide shows the mechanism on a single window.

The mechanism, on one window: the band is too tight

  • Full pool: 80% band covers 88% of this holdout
  • Recent pool: the same band covers 100%
  • One window is noisy; the 8-fold number is the honest one

Delivery mode only. The left band is the pooled one and it is too tight, so it misses more. The right band is sized to the recent spread. The single window flatters the full pool here; the 8-fold number does not.

Draw from recent errors only and it recovers to 89%

Restricting the pool to the last 120 residuals restores coverage. The band is wider, and wider is the honest answer here.

Delivery mode only. The fix is the knob: resid_tail. The band widens because it is now sized to the recent spread, not the pooled one. Coverage recovers because the pool is no longer too tight.

D. Testing a claim

H0 and H1, the test statistic, the null distribution, alpha, the decision

Twenty-four lag checks by eye become one number

Reading 24 ACF bars by eye is unreliable. A test pools them into one number.

The course moment

Day 2’s Ljung-Box slide states H0, the statistic, the chi-squared reference distribution and the decision rule on one slide. This segment builds that slide, one piece at a time.

Delivery mode only. The course moment this unlocks: 24 lag checks by eye become one number (deck 3, segment B). The next slide names the claim the test makes.

The claim is that the residuals are white noise

\(H_0\): the null

The first 24 residual autocorrelations are all zero.

\(H_1\): the alternative

At least one of them is not zero.

The null is white noise: no autocorrelation at any lag. The alternative is that some lag still has structure in it.

Delivery mode only. The null is the claim the test checks. White noise means the residuals are uncorrelated, which is what a finished model leaves behind. The next slide names the statistic that pools the lags.

The test statistic pools the squared lags

\[Q = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{r_k^2}{T-k}\]

\(r_k\): the lag-\(k\) autocorrelation · \(T\): the number of residuals · \(\ell\): the number of lags tested, 24 here

\(Q\) sums the squared autocorrelations, weighted by \(T\). One number, pooled from 24 lags.

Delivery mode only. The statistic is the pooling made explicit. Each lag’s squared autocorrelation is weighted by \(T\) and summed. The next slide names the shape \(Q\) has if the null is true.

Under the null, Q has a known shape

If \(H_0\) is true, \(Q\) follows a chi-squared distribution with 24 degrees of freedom. That shape is the reference.

Delivery mode only. The null distribution is the reference the observed \(Q\) is measured against. The next slide names alpha, which sets where the rejection region begins.

Alpha is the false-alarm rate you accept

\(\alpha = 0.05\) means you accept a 5% chance of a false alarm: rejecting \(H_0\) when it is true.

Alpha is set before the data is looked at. It is the price of a false alarm, and 0.05 is the course’s choice.

Delivery mode only. Alpha is the significance level. It is the probability of a Type I error: rejecting the null when it is true. The next slide names the critical value, which is where the top 5% begins.

The critical value is where the top 5% begins

The critical value is the 95th percentile of the null. Reject \(H_0\) if \(Q\) lands past it.

Delivery mode only. The critical value is the boundary between the acceptance and rejection regions. The next slide shows where the seasonal naive’s \(Q\) lands.

The seasonal naive’s Q lands far out in the tail

The observed \(Q\) is enormous. The residuals are not white noise; the model is not finished.

Delivery mode only. The observed \(Q\) is far out in the tail, past the critical value. The decision is to reject the null. The next slide names the warning that prevents the misreading this slide invites.

A small p-value says detectable, not large

Ljung-Box on 405 residuals finds tiny autocorrelation. A small \(p\)-value says the effect is detectable, not that it is large.

\(p\) does not rank models. The seasonal naive is the best of the benchmarks and has the smallest \(p\), because its one-step residual is a year-on-year change while the naive’s is a month-on-month one.

Delivery mode only. The warning is the point. A small \(p\)-value means the effect is detectable at this sample size, not that the effect is large. The next slide names the language that prevents the other misreading.

Failing to reject is not accepting

\(p \ge 0.05\) means the residuals are indistinguishable from white noise. It does not mean \(H_0\) is true.

The course says “indistinguishable from white noise”, not “accept \(H_0\)”. Failing to reject is not accepting.

Delivery mode only. The language matters. Failing to reject means the data did not provide strong enough evidence to rule out the null. It does not prove the null is true. The next slide names the scope of the test.

The test is a pass/fail, not a score

The Ljung-Box test says whether the model is finished. It does not say how good it is.

A pass/fail on whether a model is finished, not a score. The ranking comes from the evaluation harness, not from the \(p\)-value.

Delivery mode only. The scope is the point. The test is a gate, not a leaderboard. The next segment takes the \(p\)-value apart, because the course quotes 1e-240 as settled and the reader needs to know what that number is.

E. The p-value

What it is, read off the null distribution, and the three misreadings

The p-value: how often this extreme would happen under H0

\[p = P\big(Q \ge Q_{\text{obs}} \;\big|\; H_0\big)\]

The \(p\)-value is the probability, calculated assuming \(H_0\) is true, of obtaining a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.

\(p\) conditions on \(H_0\). It is not a statement about whether \(H_0\) is true; it is a statement about how surprising the data would be if \(H_0\) were true.

Delivery mode only. The definition is the whole slide. \(p\) is a tail area under the null distribution, measured at the observed value. The next slide shows how to read it off the null.

Read it off the null distribution, at the observed value

The \(p\)-value is the tail area beyond the observed \(Q\), under the null distribution. Here it is 9e-241.

Delivery mode only. The \(p\)-value is the tail area under the null curve to the right of the observed \(Q\). Here that tail is off the axis, so the chart marks the observed \(Q\) off-scale and states the \(p\)-value as a number. The next slide names the first misreading.

p is not the probability that H0 is true

The most common misreading: \(p\) is not \(P(H_0 \text{ is true})\).

\(p\) conditions on \(H_0\) being true. It does not measure the probability that \(H_0\) is true. Those are different questions.

Delivery mode only. The misreading is the point. \(p\) is a tail area under the null; it is not a posterior probability of the null. The next slide names the second misreading.

p is not the probability the result happened by chance

\(p\) is not \(P(\text{data})\). It is \(P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)\).

The result did not “happen by chance”. The \(p\)-value is how often a statistic this extreme would occur if \(H_0\) were true.

Delivery mode only. The second misreading. “By chance” is not a probability; it is a story. The \(p\)-value is a tail area, not a story. The next slide names the third misreading.

A small p does not mean a large effect

  • \(r = 0.12\) at \(T = 417\): p = 0.01 (detectable)
  • \(r = 0.30\) at \(T = 40\): p = 0.06 (not detectable)

Detectable is not the same as large. A tiny effect at a large sample size is detectable; a large effect at a small sample size is not.

Delivery mode only. The third misreading. The \(p\)-value depends on both the effect size and the sample size. A tiny \(r\) at 417 months is detectable; a large \(r\) at 40 months is not. The next slide is the course payoff.

A tiny p means the model is not finished, not very wrong

\(p \approx 10^{-240}\) means structure is left on the table. It does not mean the model is very wrong.

The floor is beatable. The seasonal naive’s residuals fail Ljung-Box at \(p \approx 10^{-240}\), so there is signal in this series that nothing on Day 2 captured. That is why Day 3 exists.

Delivery mode only. The course payoff. The tiny \(p\)-value is the opening for Day 3: the floor is beatable because the residuals are not white noise. The next segment maps each idea back to the course slide that needs it.
