A. Four simple models set the floor

Ch 5.1-5.4 · Fit one, diagnose it, let the fault name the next

Every formula here is built from four symbols

Symbol Meaning
\(T\) the forecast origin, the last observed time
\(h\) the forecast horizon, steps ahead
\(m\) the seasonal period, 12 for monthly data
\(\hat{y}_{a\mid b}\) the forecast of time \(a\), made from data up to time \(b\)

The origin stays at \(T\) all segment, so we drop the bar and write \(\hat{y}_{T+h}\). It comes back whenever the origin moves.

Four symbols carry the whole segment, and the room already knows two of them from Day 1. T is where we stand, h is how far ahead we look, m is the length of one cycle. Every formula in this segment uses nothing else.

Spend a sentence on the bar, because it is the only one that is new and it is the one that trips people later. Read it out loud as “of, given”: y-hat T+h given T is the forecast of the value at T+h, made knowing everything up to T. Segment A never moves the origin, so the bar is dropped there and the formulas stay short. The residual slide needs y-hat t given t-1, one step from yesterday, and the conformal segment of lecture 2 needs e t+h given t, where the origin slides. Same notation, three uses.

Three baselines start by ignoring the calendar

The mean. Average everything, use it for every horizon:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = \bar{y} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} y_t\]

The naive. Take the last observation, use it for every horizon:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_T\]

The drift. The naive, tilted by the line through the first and last point:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_T + \dfrac{h}{T-1}(y_T - y_1)\]

45 seconds each, and they are a set, not items 1-3 of a list of four: none of them has a term for the calendar, and that shared blind spot is what the next four slides are about.

The mean uses all T observations, so on a series that has grown sixfold it sits far below where the series is now. The naive uses one observation, which at least puts it at the right level. The drift is the naive with a slope bolted on - the line through the first and last observation, extended.

Point at drift’s denominator, because it returns in the next lecture: T-1 rather than T because T observations have T-1 gaps between them, and that same T-1 turns up in its interval width formula for exactly the same reason.

Read the card and stop. The room should be able to tell you, before the next slide, that none of them can produce a December.

Two of the three at least start in the right place

Point at the flat pink line first. That is the mean, and on a series that has grown sixfold it is visibly hopeless, which is the point of showing it. It is not a straw man: it is the reason a scale-free metric exists at all, and lecture 3 uses it.

The green naive line is flat too, but it starts from the right place. Then the drift, which is the only line on the slide with any growth in it - and ask the room what month the drift line thinks it is. It has no idea, and you can see that it has no idea, because it is straight.

Ask which one they would rather ship, then ask what is still wrong with the best of the three. The answer they will give, in some form, is that these are straight lines through data that is obviously not straight. Hold that thought: the next three slides turn it into a measurement.

Residuals flatter, forecast errors do not

Residual (\(e_t\)): one-step error on training data

\[e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\]

Residuals (\(e_t\))

  • In-sample training errors

  • Flattering to the model

Forecast Errors (\(e_{T+h}\))

  • Out-of-sample holdout errors

  • Honest evidence

Naive: \(e_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\), the raw one-step change.

The holdout has not happened yet. The only evidence a model has already given you is what it got wrong on the training data, and that is what we are about to read.

The bar is the one from the notation table, with the origin moved: yhat_{t|t-1} is the forecast of y_t made from data up to t-1.

Residuals flatter, forecast errors do not. Everything in this segment is residuals. Everything in lecture 3 is forecast errors.

For the naive the residual is simple subtraction: this step minus the one before. That is what makes it possible to say where the error came from, rather than only how big it was.

The naive’s errors still know what month it is

  • Lags 12 and 24 (orange) stand at 0.68 and 0.58, against a bound of 0.10
  • Day 1 named that shape: a spike every \(m\) lags is seasonality, still in there

The first diagnosis of the day and the template for every one after, so go slowly. Send them back to Day 1’s table of five ACF shapes first - it was built to read a series and reads residuals unchanged, because residuals are just another series.

Ask what shape is on the screen and wait. Somebody will say the spikes at 12 and 24, and that is the entire finding: the seasonal pattern went through the model and came out untouched.

Say the size out loud, because “significant” is doing real work. Bounds at plus or minus 0.10; the orange spikes at 0.68 and 0.58, six or seven times that. Not a marginal call.

Land the card, noting the fix was named by the diagnostic rather than guessed at. Somebody usually asks whether drift’s slope handles this - take a show of hands before you turn the slide.

Drift fixes the mean and nothing else

  • Drift’s residual is the naive’s, less the constant slope 0.77: same spread, same every lag
  • Mean residual moves from +0.77 to +0.00, and lags 12 and 24 do not move at all
  • A trend term fixed the bias. It could not fix a fault that was never about trend

Two correlograms that are the same picture - let the room hunt for the difference before you say there is not one. The only thing that moves is the number under each panel: +0.77 to +0.00. Every bar is identical.

The algebra is one line, worth the board. Drift’s fitted value is last month plus a fixed slope, so its residual is the naive’s shifted down by a constant - exactly, to machine precision. A constant shift changes the mean and nothing else, which is why the spreads match to four figures and every autocorrelation is identical.

So drift did what it was asked and zeroed the bias, and the spikes at 12 and 24 sit where they sat, because a straight line has nothing to say about December.

That closes the question the last slide opened: the tilt we had does not buy a calendar. Note what just got spent - drift is the only benchmark with a trend term and it is now behind us, which is the reason segment B exists.

The seasonal naive repeats last year’s same month

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_{T+h-m(k+1)}, \qquad k = \left\lfloor \frac{h-1}{m} \right\rfloor\]

Monthly data, so \(m = 12\): one seasonal cycle is one year.

  • \(k+1\) is how many seasonal cycles back the forecast reaches
  • \(h = 1 \dots 12\): \(k = 0\), so it copies last year’s same month
  • \(h = 13 \dots 24\): \(k = 1\), so it steps back a second year

The next lecture’s staircase is built on this \(k\): flat within each block of \(m = 12\) months, one step up per seasonal cycle.

Say m out loud before the formula: the data is monthly, so one cycle is 12 months, and m = 12 everywhere in this deck. Everything else on the slide is that substitution.

k is zero whenever the horizon stays inside one cycle, so for h up to 12 the seasonal naive is just “last year’s same month”. k only starts counting at h = 13, and that is exactly where its prediction interval takes its first step up. At h = 24 it is still 1, so the second year is copied whole: k changes once per cycle, not once per step. The interval formulas in lecture 2 use the same k for the same reason, so give this slide a real minute: the staircase later has no floor without it.

Fit four models at once in code

from statsforecast import StatsForecast
from statsforecast.models import (HistoricAverage, Naive,
                                  SeasonalNaive, RandomWalkWithDrift)

sf = StatsForecast(models=[HistoricAverage(), Naive(),
                           SeasonalNaive(season_length=12),
                           RandomWalkWithDrift()], freq=D.FREQ)
fc = sf.forecast(df=train, h=H, level=[80, 95], fitted=True)
fv = sf.forecast_fitted_values()      # the residuals we just read

Four models, about four lines each, no tuning, one second to run. This is the cell exercise 2.1 opens with.

The one argument to point at is fitted=True, because it is the difference between a forecast and a diagnosis. Without it you get fc and nothing else; with it, forecast_fitted_values gives you the in-sample fitted values whose residuals the last five slides were built on.

On many series the seasonal naive is genuinely tough to beat, and by the end of today they will have watched it beat a decomposition model over eight folds. Do not let these sound like toys.

The season is gone, but the decay is not

  • Lags 12 and 24 (orange) now fall inside the bounds at -0.03 and +0.04

Say the comparison out loud - the pair is the argument. Earlier the orange lags were 0.68 and 0.58; now -0.03 and +0.04, both inside the bounds. Students skate past that, so make it count: it is the first time today a diagnosis has been confirmed by a later measurement rather than asserted.

Now the reason drift was spent early. A missing term is named and no benchmark on the list supplies it: drift has the slope and cannot produce a December, the seasonal naive has the calendar and cannot grow, and neither patches into the other. The next model is a different kind of object, and that is segment B. Take the card from the room rather than reading it to them.

If asked why r_1 is near 1: neighbouring months share almost all of their shortfall, because the level moves slowly. It decays to nothing by lag 11 because eleven months is long enough for the level to have moved on.

B. One model with both terms

Ch 5.7, 5.4 · The decomposition route, and the verdict on all five

Decomposition is also a forecasting method

1 · STL splits the series, and the three parts group into two:

\[y_t = \underbrace{T_t + R_t}_{A_t,\;\text{seasonally adjusted}} + \underbrace{S_t}_{\text{season}}\]

2 · Forecast each part with the simple model that suits it:

\[\underbrace{\hat{A}_{T+h} = A_T + \frac{h}{T-1}(A_T - A_1)}_{\text{drift}} \qquad \underbrace{\hat{S}_{T+h} = S_{T+h-m(k+1)}}_{\text{seasonal naive}}\]

3 · Add them back:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = \hat{A}_{T+h} + \hat{S}_{T+h}\]

MSTL(season_length=12, trend_forecaster=RandomWalkWithDrift()) runs all three steps in one call.

The answer the room worked out just before the divider. Build it one fragment at a time, saying the step before showing the algebra.

Step 1 is Day 1’s decomposition regrouped: trend plus remainder is the seasonally adjusted series A_t, and S_t is everything else. If anyone reads T_t as the forecast origin, that is Day 1’s trend-cycle - subscripted t, one value per time step, not the single origin T.

Step 2 is the payoff, so point at it rather than reading it. Both formulas are benchmarks they already have with y swapped for a part, and they are the exact two the residual checks named: drift on the part with trend and no season, the seasonal naive on the part that is nothing but season. Each model is finally pointed at the component it is good at.

Step 3 adds them back, and the card says it is one constructor call.

trend_forecaster is pinned to drift on purpose - the default is AutoETS (Day 3), and no Day 3 model should quietly win a Day 2 comparison.

The plus sign assumes additive seasonality; multiplicative is handled by logging first.

STL’s two parts forecast separately, then add

The two top panels are the two diagnoses of segment A, drawn as pictures.

Ljung-Box turns 24 lag checks into one test

\(H_0\): the first \(\ell = 24\) residual autocorrelations are all zero.

\[Q = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{r_k^2}{T-k} \qquad Q \sim \chi^2_{\ell-K} \; \text{ under } H_0\]

\(r_k\): the lag-\(k\) autocorrelation · \(K\): parameters estimated, 0 here · \(p\): how often a \(Q\) this large would happen if \(H_0\) were true

  • \(p < 0.05\): autocorrelation is real \(\rightarrow\) model is not finished
  • \(p \ge 0.05\): residuals are indistinguishable from white noise

Motivation from Day 1: with 24 individual lag bounds at 95%, expecting 1 spike by chance is normal. Every ACF this segment read was read by eye, and by eye is fine when a spike is seven times the bound. Ljung-Box pools all lags into a single test so the call does not have to be made by eye: - H_0: all autocorrelations up to lag l are zero. - Rule of thumb: l = 24 for seasonal monthly data (2m), K = 0 for benchmarks (no estimated params). - p is a probability, not a size: it says how surprising this Q would be if the residuals really were white noise.

One sentence of warning before the next slide, because it prevents the misreading that slide invites: this is a pass/fail on whether a model is finished. It is not a score, and it does not rank models.

Every baseline fails, and they fail differently

  • Every \(p\) is far below 0.05: not one of the five is finished
  • Read the shapes, not the \(p\): annual spikes mean a missing season, decay means a missing trend
  • STL + drift halves the spread, 12.7 to 6.5, and still fails: see lag 24
  • The residual checklist is four: uncorrelated · zero mean · constant variance · normal

Five panels in teaching order, left to right, so the eye walks the lecture’s path. Mean fails everything. Naive leaves the orange spikes. Seasonal naive kills the spikes and leaves the decay. Drift is the naive again. STL plus drift is the only panel with neither.

Head off the misreading: a room reading a p column will rank by it. The seasonal naive has the smallest p of the four benchmarks and is the best of them - its one-step residual is a year-on-year change, the naive’s is month-on-month, so the two are not comparable. The column is pass/fail; the ranking comes from lecture 3.

Do not oversell the last panel. STL plus drift is the best-behaved residual of the day and still not white noise: p is about 7e-06, and if asked where that comes from, point at the one orange bar at lag 24 below the bound. That gap is Day 3’s opening.

Read the four verdicts off the last bullet, do not re-teach them. Uncorrelated and zero mean failed, constant variance failed, normal is untested and the next lecture opens with it. Say the split as an instruction: the first two are messages about the model, so go back and change it. Constant variance leaves the point forecast alone, so there is nothing to change - a different kind of finding, not a lesser one, and the reason one sigma cannot size a band.

A stats-literate room reaches for a transform before you offer it. Logs, or Box-Cox at the spine’s lambda of about 0.07, would take constant variance off the list. Real fix, deliberately not today’s: today is what to do when the width is wrong. Day 3 opens with the transform.
