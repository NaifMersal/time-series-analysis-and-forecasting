Open with the contrast, because the contrast is the lesson. The left block is the four lines they wrote this morning: one model, chosen by hand, scored over eight folds by their own harness. That is the right amount of code on one series, and it is the right amount because the judgment is theirs.

The right block is the same job in two calls. The first names the horizon and the metric; the second fits a list of models inside a time budget and prints a ranking. Nothing about the job changed. What changed is who picks the model and who scores it, and this segment is about exactly those two decisions.

Do not name the tool yet beyond the import line; the next three slides are the tool’s interface, and the log and the leaderboard after that are its output.

Two renames and a constructor. That is the entire conceptual content of the conversion, and it is worth showing precisely because it is so thin: the long format this course has used since the first hour is the format every serious forecasting library expects, under different names.

Say the general lesson, since it outlives this library. When you meet a new forecasting tool, the first question is what it calls the three columns and whether it wants one frame or many. If the answer is long format, which it almost always is, you already know how to feed it.

One detail the docs state plainly and it is worth ten seconds: a TimeSeriesDataFrame holds a panel of series, and every forecast is per series. There are no cross-series interactions; it is not multivariate forecasting. On the spine the panel holds exactly one item, which is why the lab can feed it the same frame everything else used.

The slice_by_timestep call in the notebook is how AutoGluon cuts a holdout. It is their train_test, with a different name.

Most of the zoo is models you have already met

Three you built: two Day 2 benchmarks and this morning’s ETS. Two you have not: ARIMA and Theta, both on the map in fifteen minutes.

Read the zoo aloud, one name at a time, and let the room sort them. Naive and SeasonalNaive are Day 2’s benchmarks. AutoETS is this morning. Those three they can read a result for without help.

AutoARIMA and Theta they cannot, and saying so is the honest version of this slide. A framework will fit whatever is in its catalogue, including families nobody has taught you, and the map in Part B is where those two get their names. That gap is not a flaw in the tool; it is the reason the map exists.

Two practical notes for the lab. The zoo is pinned to local models on purpose, so nothing neural downloads and nothing needs a GPU; the tool’s own presets reach for deep models and the two minute budget would not cover them. And time_limit is a budget, not a target, so the run stops when the clock runs out.

Warn them again about the runtime reset wiping the install.

fit() runs a queue of models under one budget

  • The zoo runs in turn, one model at a time
  • Each gets a share of the remaining time_limit
  • A WeightedEnsemble is fitted on top, by default
  • The ranking rests on one internal validation split

The docs’ presets run from fast_training to best_quality. This lab pins the zoo instead, so every row that comes back is a model you can read.

Say what fit does, in order, because the order is the design. The models run one after another in the order the zoo gives them. Each is handed a share of whatever budget is left, which is why the ensemble fitted last gets whatever survived, and why a slow model starves the ones behind it. The next slide shows the arithmetic in the tool’s own log.

The last bullet is the one that pays off in the two slides after it: all of this is ranked on a single internal validation split. The tool has the machinery for more windows; the lab leaves it at one on purpose, so the leaderboard slide can show the gap.

One line to save for the Colab run: the docs list the exponential smoothing model under the zoo key ETS, while the lab writes AutoETS after statsforecast. If the fit complains about an unknown model name, that is the first line to change, and changing it is a useful lesson about reading a tool’s catalogue.

Set the frame before the log: this is the tool’s own quick start, run on its M4 hourly example, not on our spine. The numbers are not ours, and that is fine, because the shape is what is on the board.

Walk the log top to bottom. First line of interest: the tool announces that it has negated the metric so that higher is better, and it tells you how to get the real value back, multiply by minus one. That sentence is in every AutoGluon run, and it is the first thing to check whenever a tool’s ranking surprises you.

Next, one line per model: the remaining budget it was given, and one validation score. Note the budget arithmetic: each model gets an equal share of what is left, so the ensemble that is fitted last gets whatever survived.

Last block: the ensemble. It is not a mystery model; the log prints its weights, a plain weighted average of the members, chosen to be best on the same one validation window. If the room asks what the ensemble is, this line is the answer.

The tool’s own quick start, M4 hourly. Higher is better: every score is negated, so -1.97 is a MASE of 1.97.

score_val is one validation window. Day 2 spent an hour on why that cannot rank two models. The verdict comes from your harness.

Same run, now as the table the lab will show them. The full table has fit times and fit order as well; three columns are on the board because those are the three that carry the lesson.

The sign: every score is negated, so higher is better. A score_val of -1.97 is a MASE of 1.97. Say it out loud once with a number, because the sign flip is the trap that catches people who have used the library for months.

The window: score_val comes from one internal validation split, and predictor.leaderboard(tsdf), the call the lab makes, adds score_test, one more window. That is why both columns are on the board.

The swap: between validation and test, RecursiveTabular and Toto2 change places on this same example. The tool’s own two windows disagree about which of the two is better. That is not a flaw to work around; it is the reason the ranking is a shortlist, and the next move is to cross-validate the top of it properly, on your harness, over your eight folds.

The ensemble row beats its members on both windows here. On other data it will not. Note that and move on; it is Part B’s ensembles line arriving early.

What the framework buys, and what it does not

Buys

  • Five models fitted, tuned and ensembled inside one call and a fixed budget
  • The same code on 148 series, with no new loop
  • A shortlist in two minutes

Does not buy

  • An evaluation you can trust: one window, one sign
  • A reason the winner beats your floor
  • Any read on whether its bands are too wide

You have now seen every line this library runs. In industry you hand this step to a framework, and you still own the judgement.

This is the thesis of the segment, so land it slowly.

The left column is real and should not be undersold. Five models, tuned and ensembled, inside a fixed budget, with the same code working on 148 series instead of one. On a panel that size orchestration is not optional, and nobody should leave thinking the course disapproves of the tool.

The right column is what the last three days were for. The tool will always return a winner. It will not tell you whether the winner is better than a seasonal naive, whether its intervals are honest, or whether the margin survives a second window. Those are the questions, and they are yours.

The card is the line to close on. Say it, then hand over to the map.

B. The field, and where to go next

Listed, not taught · one line each

Four more classical families, by name

  • Theta · simple, fast, strong. In AutoGluon’s zoo, and best on this series
  • ARIMA · Ch 9. The other classical family, and where the structure Ljung-Box found on Day 2 actually lives
  • Dynamic regression · Ch 7 and 10, for when you have price or promotions
  • Hierarchical · Ch 11, when state totals must equal the national total

Four names, one line each, and no apology for the brevity. The purpose of the map is recognition: when one of these turns up in a paper or a job spec, they should know roughly what it is and where to read about it.

Theta first, because it was in the zoo ten minutes ago, it is in the capstone in fifteen, and it makes an honest point about complexity: a very simple method that is about to beat everything the course taught properly.

ARIMA next, and be straight about why it is a line on a map rather than a segment. It costs differencing, stationarity, AR against MA, and PACF for order selection, which is a diagnostic Day 1 never showed. That is a chapter, and three hours does not have one to spend. What it is worth knowing is that the autocorrelation Ljung-Box found in the seasonal naive’s residuals yesterday is exactly what an MA term consumes. Ch 9 is written to be read on its own.

Dynamic regression is the one most likely to matter in their actual work. Everything this course did used only the series’ own past. The moment you have a price, a promotion calendar or a holiday flag, that is the chapter. Note that Ch 10 is really Ch 7 plus Ch 9, so regression travels with it.

Hierarchical is worth naming for anyone forecasting a business with regions or product lines, which is most of them.

The harness is the part that transfers

  • Lag-feature ML · LightGBM on lags and rolling stats; no fpppy chapter
  • Global neural · DeepAR, Transformers. One model across many series
  • Foundation models · Chronos, Moirai, TimeGPT. Pretrained, zero fitting
  • Ensembles · combine forecasts; AutoGluon’s WeightedEnsemble is one

Every one of these still gets scored by the harness you built. That is the part that transfers.

The second half of the map, and the half with the most hype attached, so keep the tone level.

Lag features are how most working data scientists actually do this: build a tabular frame of lags and rolling statistics, then fit a gradient booster. fpppy has no chapter on it. The zoo log from Part A already showed the two AutoGluon versions of it, RecursiveTabular and DirectTabular, so the name has a face. Warn them once about the trap Day 2 covered, which is computing a feature over data the fold was not allowed to see.

Neural models are global: one model across many series, learning shared structure. On one series with 441 points they are the wrong tool, which is worth saying plainly because the instinct runs the other way.

Foundation models are the genuinely new thing. Pretrained on many series, and they forecast a series they have never seen with no fitting at all. On Colab, Chronos-Bolt-small or Moirai-small run on CPU; TimeGPT needs an API key and Moirai needs weights from Hugging Face.

The newest of them have moved past one series at a time. TimesFM 3 is pretrained for multivariate forecasting and accepts past and known-future covariates, so a promotion calendar or a holiday flag arrives with no fitting step at all. That is the cross-link at the bottom of these notes, minus the fit. Google’s write-up is at https://research.google/blog/timesfm-3-a-zero-shot-foundation-model-for-multivariate-forecasting/ and it reports the top of GIFT-Eval and FEV-Bench among foundation models. Quote the ranking, then say what to do with it, because that is the part they own: a leaderboard is somebody else’s series and somebody else’s folds. They have a harness, so the question is whether it beats their own seasonal naive on their own data. That is the closing chart of this deck, pointed at a model nobody in the room has run.

Ensembles last, because it is the cheapest reliable win in forecasting and the least glamorous, and they have now seen one with its weights printed in a log.

One cross-link worth making if the room is with you: the covariates support in AutoGluon, known covariates like promotions and holidays, is where dynamic regression lives inside a framework. The chapter name and the framework flag are the same idea.

The floor held, and one thing beat it

The closing result

Theta wins on MASE and on scaled CRPS, in three lines and with no lecture. Nothing this course taught properly beat the seasonal naive on the distribution.

  • A one-line benchmark survived a full day of fitted models
  • Complexity did not order the results; honest uncertainty did
  • You cannot know which is which without a harness

Two things to land, and they pull in opposite directions on purpose.

First, the floor held. Across ETS and the decomposition route, a model that takes one line and no fitting time was better calibrated than both. This is why the benchmark floor is the first thing you build on a new series. Not as a formality. As the thing that most of your candidates will fail to beat.

Second, the one model that did beat it is the simple one. Theta is simpler than ETS, got no lecture at all, and won on both metrics. If complexity ordered forecast accuracy, this chart would read the other way around.

What did order it was whether each model’s uncertainty was priced honestly, and that is not visible from the model, only from the evaluation. Which is the third bullet, and the whole point of Day 2.

Then hand to the lab: the capstone is where they score a model of their own against this same floor, and Theta is one of the options.
