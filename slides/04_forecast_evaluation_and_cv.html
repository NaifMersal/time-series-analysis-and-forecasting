A. Scoring a point forecast

Ch 5.8 · Point forecast metrics

Only a scaled metric can compare across series

Forecast error: \(e_{T+h} = y_{T+h} - \hat{y}_{T+h|T}\)

Metric Property Limitation
MAE Easy to interpret Scale-dependent: cannot be pooled across series
RMSE Punishes large errors Scale-dependent
MAPE Unit-free Explodes near zero, and asymmetric
MASE Scaled: MAE over the benchmark’s Needs the seasonal period
RMSSE Scaled: RMSE over the benchmark’s Same, and still punishes outliers

MAE and RMSE work well within a single series. To compare across different products or series, use MASE.

Rescaling the data moves MAE but not MASE

Same series, new unit, with \(k > 0\): \(y'_t = k\,y_t\) and \(\hat{y}'_t = k\,\hat{y}_t\).

\[\text{MAE}' = \frac{1}{n}\sum \lvert k y_t - k\hat{y}_t \rvert = k\,\text{MAE} \quad\text{(moves with the unit)}\]

\[PE'_t = \frac{k y_t - k\hat{y}_t}{k y_t} = PE_t \qquad \text{MASE}' = \frac{k\,\text{MAE}}{k\,Q} = \text{MASE}\]

Now shift as well as scale, \(y'_t = k y_t + c\), which is what °C to °F is. Differences kill \(c\), so MASE holds. MAPE’s denominator keeps it.

MAPE cancelled \(k\) and is still the worst metric in this deck. Cancelling is necessary, not sufficient.

Do this one at the board pace, not the slide pace. Ask what happens to each metric if the finance team reports the same series in thousands instead of dollars. Everyone knows MAE has to move. Then substitute.

MAE picks up the k and moves with the unit. MAPE cancels it, top and bottom. MASE cancels it too, and the reason is worth saying out loud: Q is built from the same data, so the denominator scales by exactly the same k as the numerator. That cancellation is the licence for what Day 1 did when it pooled 148 retail series with different turnovers into one ranking.

Then the sting. MAPE passed the test and is still the worst metric in this deck, so cancelling k is necessary and not sufficient. Add an offset and the two part company: every term in MASE is a difference, so c disappears, but MAPE divides by a level and the c rides along. That is the temperature objection, three slides early, and now it is a consequence rather than an assertion.

Read the two forecasts off the chart before quoting the numbers, and let the room commit. Forecast A is two units too high everywhere; forecast B is fifteen hundredths too low everywhere. Everyone picks B, and everyone is right.

Then show the MAPE column: A scores 304%, B scores 23%. MAPE agrees here. Now ask what happens when the actual is 0.2 and you are two units out - the percentage error is a thousand percent, and it is the denominator doing that, not the forecast.

Three reasons MAPE cannot select a model

  1. Undefined at zero: explodes on intermittent / low-volume demand
  2. Asymmetric: penalizes over-forecasting far more than under-forecasting
  3. Fails without a true zero: the offset never cancels, so °C breaks it

The second one surprises people, so make it concrete. Forecast 100 when the actual is 50 and MAPE charges you 100%. Forecast 50 when the actual is 100 and it charges you 50%. The same 50-unit error, twice the penalty for being high. A team that selects on MAPE learns to under-forecast, and in an inventory setting that means stocking out.

The third is the one people argue with, and the rescaling slide already answered it. MAPE needs a meaningful zero to divide by, and 0 degrees Celsius is not “no temperature”, it is a point on a scale somebody chose. Convert to Fahrenheit and the offset survives in the denominator, so the same forecast scores differently in the two units. MASE, built from differences, does not care.

None of this makes MAPE useless. It reports well to a business audience. It just cannot be the thing you select a model on.

Report MAPE to executives if required, but never optimize models using MAPE.

Dividing by the benchmark makes errors comparable

Error divided by the in-sample Seasonal Naive error (\(Q\)):

\[\text{MASE} = \frac{\text{MAE}}{Q}, \qquad Q = \frac{1}{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T} \lvert y_t - y_{t-m} \rvert\]

\(Q\) is measured in the units of the data, which is what cancelled the \(k\).

  • \(\text{MASE} < 1\) \(\rightarrow\) Better than the seasonal naive baseline
  • \(\text{MASE} = 1\) \(\rightarrow\) Exactly as good as seasonal naive
  • \(\text{MASE} > 1\) \(\rightarrow\) Worse than simple baseline

The denominator is the whole idea: divide by the in-sample one-step seasonal naive error, and the metric becomes scale-free and readable. MASE below 1 beats the benchmark, above 1 loses to it.

Warn them the seasonal naive will score above 1 on this data, and that this is not a contradiction: forecasting 24 months out is harder than the one-step in-sample errors sitting in the denominator.

RMSSE is the same idea as MASE with squared errors, which is why the two columns agree on the ranking here and why both end up on the leaderboard.

B. One split is not evidence

Ch 5.10 · Rolling-origin cross-validation

One split gives a number with no error bar

  • A single 24-month split is just one sample point
  • Score depends heavily on economic conditions during that window
  • High variance (\(\pm 16\%\) error bars on 24 observations)
  • Encourages data snooping and overfitting

Time series cannot use standard k-fold. We need rolling origins.

This is the hinge of the day. One split gives one number, and one number has no error bar, so it cannot support a ranking.

Ask the room how confident they would be in a drug trial with one patient. Same objection, same fix: more origins.

Rolling the origin turns one score into a distribution

# 144 periods, initial train 48, horizon 12, step 12 -> 8 origins.
P.cv_staircase(n_obs=144, initial=48, horizon=12, step=12)

Each fold trains only on past data. No fold ever peeks into its future.

Walk the diagram fold by fold. Blue is what the model saw, orange is what it was scored on, grey is the future it was not allowed to know about. Every row moves the boundary forward and never backwards.

Say explicitly why k-fold is wrong here: shuffling puts future rows in the training set, which is leakage, and the last slide of this deck comes back to it.

Three knobs control the CV: h, step_size, n_windows

# Rolling-origin CV on the spine:
cv = sf.cross_validation(
    df=spine,
    h=12,          # Forecast horizon matched to business need
    step_size=12,  # Steps between successive origins
    n_windows=8,   # Number of evaluation folds
    level=[80],
)
  • h: match the true operational planning horizon
  • step_size: frequency of forecast production
  • n_windows: number of independent test origins

Pick h from the decision, not from the data. If the plan is quarterly, h is three, and a model that wins at h=12 is not the one you want.

The trade in n_windows is honest: more windows is a steadier estimate and a shorter first training set. Eight is what this series affords.

Coverage checks the band. It cannot rank.

Ch 5.9 · Scoring the whole distribution

  • Coverage answers one question: is the 80% band honest?
  • It says nothing about width: an interval of \(\pm\infty\) covers 100%
  • Two models at 80% coverage can be worlds apart in usefulness
  • A proper score charges you for width and for misses, in one number

To score a distribution, we start by scoring a single quantile.

The infinity example lands immediately: an interval of plus or minus infinity has 100% coverage and zero value. Coverage is a pass/fail check on honesty, not a ranking metric. To rank models, we need a score that prices both width and misses fairly.

Quantile loss generalizes MAE to any level

A quantile forecast can miss low (\(y > \hat{q}_\alpha\)) or miss high (\(y < \hat{q}_\alpha\)). At \(\alpha = 0.9\) the two must not cost the same.

  • Plot
  • Formula

\[L_\alpha(\hat{q}, y) = \begin{cases} \alpha \, (y - \hat{q}) & \text{if } y \ge \hat{q} \quad \text{(underestimated)} \\ (1 - \alpha) \, (\hat{q} - y) & \text{if } y < \hat{q} \quad \text{(overestimated)} \end{cases}\]

At \(\alpha = 0.5\) both slopes are \(0.5\): MAE was quantile loss all along.

Ask the room first: if you forecast the 90th percentile, how often should the actual value come in below your forecast? Ninety percent of the time! So being higher than actual is not a failure, it is the job. The penalty must reflect that expected asymmetry.

Then notice the slopes on the chart: at alpha=0.5, both sides rise at rate 0.5. That is plain absolute error divided by 2. When you minimize MAE, you are finding the median forecast.

At alpha=0.9, the slope to the right is 0.9 and to the left is 0.1. Setting the expected derivative to zero gives P(Y <= q) = alpha.

Winkler score combines two quantiles into an interval

An \(80\%\) prediction interval \([\ell_{0.2}, u_{0.2}]\) is bounded by the \(10\%\) and \(90\%\) quantiles.

\[\text{Winkler}([\ell, u], y) = \frac{L_{0.1}(\ell, y) + L_{0.9}(u, y)}{0.1}\]

Expanding the pinball losses gives a simple, intuitive charge:

\[\text{Winkler} = \begin{cases} (u - \ell) & \text{inside the band} \\ (u - \ell) + \frac{2}{\alpha} (\ell - y) & \text{missed low } (y < \ell) \\ (u - \ell) + \frac{2}{\alpha} (y - u) & \text{missed high } (y > u) \end{cases}\]

Winkler prices the trade: narrow intervals win inside the band, but pay a heavy linear fine for every unit outside.

The Winkler score is not a new ad-hoc formula. It is literally the sum of the two bounding quantile losses divided by alpha. When the actual observation falls inside the band, the score is simply the interval width (u - l). If it falls outside, the penalty is proportional to the distance of the miss.

Scaled CRPS averages quantile loss across all levels

  • Quantile loss: scores a single quantile \(\hat{q}_\alpha\)
  • Winkler score: scores an interval \([\ell_\alpha, u_\alpha]\) (a pair of quantiles)
  • CRPS: scores the whole distribution by averaging quantile loss over all \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\)

\[\text{CRPS} = \int_0^1 2 \, L_\alpha(\hat{q}_\alpha, y) \, d\alpha \quad \approx \quad \frac{1}{|\text{levels}|} \sum_{\alpha} 2 \, L_\alpha(\hat{q}_\alpha, y)\]

Scaled CRPS divides by the seasonal naive benchmark error, making it scale-free and sortable across the entire portfolio.

This completes the arc from point forecast to distribution: MAE -> Quantile Loss -> Winkler Score -> CRPS -> Scaled CRPS. Now the room understands why LEVELS in scoring.py has a ladder of five levels (20, 40, 60, 80, 95): it provides 10 quantiles to approximate this integral.

This is the Ch 5.9 payoff, so give it the time. It is the slide that stops “my intervals are tighter” from being an argument.

The STL route has the narrower 80% band - about 40 units against the seasonal naive’s 49 - and it loses on CRPS, 0.034 to 0.031, because those narrow bands miss more often: 61% coverage against 77%. Tighter is only better if you were right, and CRPS is what prices that trade in one number.

The third one is the next slide, run live.

Always fit scalers, imputers, and feature engineering strictly on each fold’s training history. The third cause is the one that gets past code review, because nobody wrote a line that looks wrong - they just kept trying models until the test score improved. The next slide runs that one, and the lab’s stretch exercise prices the leak the students can see: a 24% discount bought by choosing the winner fold by fold.

Choosing per fold with the answer in hand buys 24%

# one rule fixed in advance, against the best model in each fold
honest = per_window["SeasonalNaive"].mean()
oracle = per_window[MODELS].min(axis=1).mean()

print(f"seasonal naive, every fold  : MASE {honest:.2f}")
print(f"best model, chosen per fold : MASE {oracle:.2f}")
print(f"discount bought by peeking  : {1 - oracle / honest:.0%}")
seasonal naive, every fold  : MASE 1.18
best model, chosen per fold : MASE 0.90
discount bought by peeking  : 24%

No rule available before a fold produces the second number. It is the holdout leaking into the choice of model.

Run this one live. The seasonal naive wins four folds and the STL route wins the other four, and swapping between them fold by fold takes the average from 1.18 to 0.90 - a 24% improvement bought entirely by knowing the answer first.

Ask the room what rule they would have written in advance to make those swaps. There isn’t one. That is the whole definition of leakage: a number produced by a decision that could not have been made at forecast time.

This is also the honest version of what happened in lecture 1, where a single window crowned the STL route at 0.70.

Four ways an honest team still fools itself

  • Regime changes: verify if old folds disagree with recent folds
  • Underpowered sample: evaluate over sufficient origins (\(>50\) points)
  • Overlapping folds: step_size < h reuses points, so intervals read too narrow
  • Data gaps: verify timestamp continuity across all origins

None of these require anyone to be careless. Every one of them has shipped from a competent team.

If you have a war story, this is where it goes. If not, the honest version is the one this course just walked through: a single window crowned the STL route at 0.70 and eight windows handed it back.
