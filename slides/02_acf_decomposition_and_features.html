The Solution

A. Autocorrelation

Ch 2.8–2.9 · The central diagnostic

ACF is correlation with your own past

Quantifies linear correlation between \(y_t\) and its lag \(y_{t-k}\):

\[r_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(y_t - \bar{y})(y_{t-k} - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t - \bar{y})^2}\]

  • \(r_k \in [-1, 1]\)
  • \(r_1\) \(\rightarrow\) correlation with previous month (inertia/trend)
  • \(r_{12}\) \(\rightarrow\) correlation with same month last year (seasonality)
  • Plot \(r_k\) against \(k\) and you have a correlogram, the ACF plot

What a correlation looks like

If you have ever done a scatterplot of x against y, you have done this. The only new thing is that x and y are the same column, offset by k.

The link goes to an uncounted refresher at the end of the deck, four scatters at four values of r. Take it if the room hesitates here, and skip it if the formula landed: the rest of the segment reads r off a plot rather than computing one.

Bounds separate real correlation from luck

White noise: no autocorrelation at any lag, every value independent of every other. For such a series:

\[r_k \sim N\left(0,\ \tfrac{1}{T}\right)\]

  • 95% of autocorrelations fall inside \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}\)
  • A spike inside the bounds is indistinguishable from noise
  • With 36 lags, ~1–2 spikes outside the bounds can occur by chance

Why the bound has a \(\sqrt{T}\) in it

Reading any ACF in five shapes

Visual Pattern Underlying Property
Slow, smooth decay Trend
Repeating peaks at \(m, 2m, 3m\) Seasonality (period \(m\))
Decay + Seasonal peaks Trend + Seasonality
All spikes inside bounds White Noise (no remaining signal)
Slow oscillating wave Cycle (no fixed period)

Key application: When the unexplained variation is white noise, the model has captured all systematic structure.

B. Decomposition

Ch 3 · Separating trend, season, and noise

A log transform flattens the growing swing

  • Plot
  • Code
# Dataset: Victorian retail takeaway turnover (spine = D.spine())
# Log transform: a multiplicative series becomes additive on the log scale
yt = np.log(spine["y"].to_numpy())

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 2.8))
P.plot_series(spine, ax=axes[0], title="Original: expanding swing", ylabel="turnover ($M)")
P.plot_series(spine.assign(y=yt), ax=axes[1], color=P.ORANGE,
              ylabel="log turnover", title="Log scale: constant swing")
  • When the swing grows with the level, take a log transform before decomposing
  • On the log scale a multiplicative series is additive: the swing stops growing
  • Back-transforming returns the median, not the mean (Ch 5.6)

Deck 1 named the diagnosis: the swing grows with the level, so the series is multiplicative, and the fix is the log transform. This slide is that fix, applied: the annual swing is now a constant size, and the sixfold trend has become a near-straight climb. Everything after this slide in the segment runs on the log-transformed series.

Box-Cox is the generalised version: a family of transforms indexed by lambda, with the log at lambda = 0. For this series the optimal lambda is about 0.07, so the log is the honest choice. Do not teach Box-Cox today; it comes back on Day 3 with ETS, where a fitted lambda is part of the model.

The median-vs-mean point is the one they will trip over in production: if a regional total is the sum of 40 back-transformed store forecasts, that total is biased low unless you correct it.

A centred moving average estimates the trend-cycle

\[\hat{T}_t = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=-k}^{k} y_{t+j} \qquad (m = 2k + 1)\]

  • Code

    title="Spine, last 15 years: the order $m$ decides what survives")

Set the window to one seasonal period: \(m = 3\) keeps the season, \(m = 12\) erases it.

The 3-MA still wobbles because three months is a quarter of a cycle, so it averages a high month with two lower ones and keeps the shape. Twelve covers every month once. Twelve is even and has no middle month, which is the wrinkle the next slide deals with, so do not use the name 2x12-MA yet.

A whole-cycle window cancels the season

Add up \(m = 2k+1\) terms of \(y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t\), centred on \(t\):

\[\sum_{j=-k}^{k} y_{t+j} \;=\; \sum_{j=-k}^{k} T_{t+j} \;+\; \sum_{j=-k}^{k} S_{t+j} \;+\; \sum_{j=-k}^{k} R_{t+j}\]

Divide by \(m\) and each component gets its own average:

\[\hat{T}_t = \underbrace{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=-k}^{k} T_{t+j}}_{\text{trend}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=-k}^{k} S_{t+j}}_{\text{season}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=-k}^{k} R_{t+j}}_{\text{noise}}\]

  • Trend survives: a symmetric window is unbiased on a locally linear trend
  • Season is exactly 0: the window spans one whole cycle (next slide)
  • Noise shrinks by \(1/m\), damped but never removed

Work left to right. The moving average is a sum, and a sum passes straight through the additive model, so the window average of y is the window average of T plus that of S plus that of R. Nothing has been assumed yet.

Then read the three terms. The trend term: over a window this short the trend is close to a straight line, and a symmetric window of a straight line averages to its midpoint, so this term is about T_t. The noise term: m independent draws averaged, so its spread is smaller by root m, damped but never gone. The season term is the interesting one and gets the next slide.

For a multiplicative model the same idea multiplies instead: going through a full cycle, S1 x S2 x … x Sm = 1, so a geometric mean over the window removes the season.

Seasonal terms always sum to zero over \(m\) steps

By construction. A seasonal component is defined to average to zero across one cycle:

\[S_1 + S_2 + \dots + S_m = 0\]

  • Without it the split is not unique: any constant inside \(S\) really belongs to \(T\)
  • With it, \(S\) is a pure swing and \(T\) alone carries the level

By periodicity (\(S_{t+m} = S_t\)), any \(m\) consecutive terms are that same full set:

\[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=-k}^{k} S_{t+j} = \frac{1}{m}(\underbrace{S_1 + S_2 + \dots + S_m}_{\text{sums to } 0}) = \mathbf{0}\]

True at every \(t\), wherever the window starts.

Two separate facts, and students conflate them. The first is a convention we impose to make the decomposition identifiable: shift 5 out of S and into T and the fit is unchanged, so the seasonal component is pinned down only once we demand it average to zero over a cycle. Classical decomposition enforces it explicitly, in the centring step of stage 3.

The second is arithmetic: periodicity means a window of length m sees each of the m seasonal values exactly once, in some rotated order, and a sum does not care about order. So the window sum is S1 + … + Sm, which the constraint makes 0. Ask what happens if the window is 11 months or 13 instead: one month gets counted twice, the sum is no longer 0, and a residue of the season leaks into the trend estimate.

An even \(m\) needs the 2×\(m\)-MA to stay centred

Twelve months have no middle month: their average sits at \(t + \tfrac{1}{2}\). Average two adjacent 12-MAs to land back on \(t\):

\[\hat{T}_t = \tfrac{1}{24}y_{t-6} + \tfrac{1}{12}\sum_{j=-5}^{5} y_{t+j} + \tfrac{1}{24}y_{t+6}\]

  • Plot
  • Code
# 2x12 Centred Moving Average filter weights:
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.5, 1.7))
P.ma_weight_plot(12, ax=ax, title="13 weights, symmetric about $t$")

\(y_{t-6}\) and \(y_{t+6}\) are the same calendar month, so between them it still gets \(\tfrac{1}{12}\), like every other month. The season cancels exactly.

The two half weights land on the same calendar month, six months either side of t. Between them that month still gets a full 1/12, like every other month, so the season still cancels exactly. That is the only reason the 2x12 form exists.

Classical decomposition is averages all the way down

  1. \(\hat{T}_t\) is the 2×12-MA
  2. Detrend: \(y_t - \hat{T}_t\)
  3. \(\hat{S}\) averages the detrended values within each month, then centres them to sum to zero
  4. \(\hat{R}_t = y_t - \hat{T}_t - \hat{S}_t\)

Every piece is a moving average or a group mean. No fitting, no parameters. That simplicity costs something.

No fitting, no parameters, nothing to tune. That is its appeal and also its ceiling, which the next slide shows.

The other cost of classical decomposition: monthly means impose one seasonal shape on all 37 years. STL, next, fixes both problems at once.

STL (Seasonal-Trend using Loess) splits all three

  • Plot
  • Code
# Dataset: Log-transformed spine turnover (yt = np.log of D.spine()["y"])
res = STL(yt, period=12, robust=True).fit()
dcmp = spine.assign(
    **{"transformed turnover": yt},
    trend=np.asarray(res.trend),
    seasonal=np.asarray(res.seasonal),
    remainder=np.asarray(res.resid),
)
fig, axes = P.decomposition_plot(
    dcmp, ["transformed turnover", "trend", "seasonal", "remainder"],
    "STL decomposition of the transformed spine")

STL bends where classical decomposition cannot

  • Adaptive: seasonal pattern can evolve smoothly over time
  • Flexible trend: estimates non-linear trend curves
  • Outlier-resistant: unusual spikes do not distort the trend (robust=True)
  • Remainder: isolates pure unexplained variation

STL = Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess. Key parameters: period (must provide) and seasonal window.

C. Time Series Features

Ch 4 · Summarising thousands of series

One row per series makes a portfolio tractable

  • In practice, you manage hundreds or thousands of series
  • Manual visual inspection is impossible
  • Solution: Compute summary features (1 row per series)

Definition

A feature is a numerical summary of a whole series (e.g. trend strength, seasonal strength, entropy).

Two strengths summarize a whole series

Using STL component variances:

\[F_T = \max\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{\text{Var}(R_t)}{\text{Var}(T_t + R_t)}\right) \qquad F_S = \max\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{\text{Var}(R_t)}{\text{Var}(S_t + R_t)}\right)\]

  • Values fall in \([0, 1]\)
  • \(F_T \approx 0\) \(\rightarrow\) No trend | \(F_T \approx 1\) \(\rightarrow\) Strong trend
  • \(F_S \approx 0\) \(\rightarrow\) No seasonality | \(F_S \approx 1\) \(\rightarrow\) Strong seasonality

Both are one minus a variance ratio. If the remainder is nearly as variable as the trend plus remainder, the trend is adding nothing and F_T is near zero. If removing the component barely changes the variance, the component was not there.

Only seasonal strength separates the portfolio

  • Plot
  • Code
# Dataset: 148 Australian retail monthly series (allr = D.retail_all())
def stl_features(g):
    y = np.log(np.clip(g["y"].to_numpy(), 1e-6, None))
    r = STL(y, period=12, robust=True).fit()
    R, T_, S = np.asarray(r.resid), np.asarray(r.trend), np.asarray(r.seasonal)
    vr = np.var(R)
    return pd.Series({
        "trend_strength": max(0.0, 1 - vr / np.var(T_ + R)),
        "seasonal_strength": max(0.0, 1 - vr / np.var(S + R)),
    })

allr = D.retail_all()
feat = allr.groupby("unique_id")[["y"]].apply(stl_features, include_groups=False).reset_index()

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9.5, 4.3))
P.feature_scatter(feat, ax=ax, marks=[(hi, P.ORANGE, "most seasonal"),
                                      (mine, P.BLACK, "the spine"),
                                      (lo, P.PINK, "least seasonal")],
                  title="148 Australian retail series, both axes on the full 0 to 1 scale")

Every one of the 148 scores above 0.89 on trend strength and 97% score above 0.96, so trend tells you nothing about which series is which. Seasonal strength runs from 0.26 to 0.98, and that is the axis worth acting on.

Features route a portfolio to the right model

  • Triage: focus manual attention on unusual series
  • Model Routing: skip seasonal models if \(F_S \approx 0\)
  • Anomaly Detection: feature outliers often indicate data errors
  • Clustering: group similar series for global models

Golden Rule

When a point looks anomalous in feature space, always plot the raw series.

Reminder: a correlation is a shape

Two numbers measured on the same thing. Multiply each one’s distance from its own average: same side, positive; opposite sides, negative. Average that, scale by the spreads, and \(r\) is what comes out.

\(r\) measures one thing: how close the cloud is to a straight line. \(+1\) and \(-1\) are the two perfect lines, \(0\) is no line at all. Back

Off the flow rather than hidden: it sits after the closing slide, so nothing in the talk walks into it, and it is not counted in the slide numbers. You get here by clicking the link on the ACF slide. Back returns to wherever you came from, and Alt+Left does the same.

Say the top line before the panels, because it is the half the ACF slide never states. Two numbers on the same thing, and the question is whether they sit on the same side of their own averages at the same time. Multiply the two deviations: both above, positive; both below, negative times negative, also positive; one of each, negative. Average that and scale it, and the answer is between -1 and +1 no matter what the units were.

Point back at the ACF formula while you say it. That numerator is exactly this product, and the only thing the ACF changes is that the second number is the same series 12 months ago.

Then the panels: four clouds, one generator, and the only thing that changes is r. Read them left to right and the room gets its calibration: 0.95 is a line with a bit of scatter, 0.5 is a trend you would argue about, 0.0 is a blob, and negative just means the line goes the other way.

The two things to say out loud. r only sees straight lines, so a perfect curve can score near zero. And r says nothing about the slope: a steep line and a shallow one both score 1.

Then make the one connection that matters here: the ACF is this same number, computed on a scatter of the series against itself k steps back. Nothing else is new.

Reminder: a statistic moves on its own

Every one of these series is pure noise, so the true \(r_1\) is \(0\). The spread is the measurement, not the series. Back

Off the flow, after the closing slide, reached by the link on the bounds slide. Back returns there.

This is the one idea behind every square root of n in the course, so it is worth the sixty seconds. Two thousand series, every one of them white noise by construction, so the true lag-1 autocorrelation is exactly zero. Measure it on 441 months anyway and you get the histogram: almost never zero, usually within about a tenth of it.

The dashed lines are the bound from the slide, and about 95% of the simulations land inside them. That is what the bound is: not a claim about this series, but the range luck alone produces when there is nothing there.

So a spike inside the bound is not evidence of no correlation, and it is not evidence of correlation either. It is a spike you cannot tell apart from noise. Longer series, narrower bound, because the square root of T is in the denominator.
