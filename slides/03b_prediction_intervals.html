A. Prediction intervals

Ch 5.5 · Quantifying uncertainty

The future is a range, not a point

At origin \(T\) the future value is still random; capital \(Y\) marks a value not yet observed:

\[Y_{T+h}\mid \mathcal{I}_T \qquad \mathcal{I}_T = \text{everything known at time } T\]

  • Point forecast: one centre, usually a mean or median
  • 80% interval: the \(0.1\) and \(0.9\) quantiles of \(Y_{T+h}\mid \mathcal{I}_T\)
  • 95% interval: the \(0.025\) and \(0.975\) quantiles

The distribution belongs to the unknown future value, not to the forecast. What a quantile is

Decisions like staffing buffers, warehouse capacity, and safety inventory depend directly on the interval width. We produce one point forecast, but we do not believe the future must equal that point. Before it is observed, we represent the future as plausible outcomes conditional on what we know now. The point forecast is one summary; an interval takes two quantiles. The rest of the deck asks how to construct them.

The three methods differ in how they get the quantiles: impose a Gaussian shape, simulate futures from empirical errors, or calibrate a set from past forecast errors. The point forecast stays the same throughout; only the band changes.

The seasonal naive’s prediction interval

Make the room read the dashed actual line against the bands rather than admiring the shading. Ask how many of the 24 points fall inside the 80% band. It looks excellent here, and lecture 3 shows a single 24-point window cannot measure a coverage rate at all.

One sentence if you want one: the interval is the forecast, and the line down the middle is just its most likely value.

Plant one thing without explaining it - the band is flat through 2017 then steps out at the start of 2018. Point, say “we will come back to that”, move on. Two slides later the recursion says why, and the room recognizes it rather than being told.

Every model says: something known, plus an error

Lecture 1 defined a residual as a subtraction:

\[e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\]

Move the forecast across, and the same line becomes a recipe for making data:

\[y_t = \hat{y}_{t|t-1} + e_t\]

Every interval method in this deck is one answer to the same question: where does \(e_t\) come from?

One line of algebra the room can do in its head, so do it in front of them. Write the residual definition, move the forecast across, stop.

Read the second line as a recipe: any value is the forecast we could have made plus the error we then made. True by construction for every model here, which is why what follows applies to all five, not just the seasonal naive.

The card is the map for the next twenty minutes - put it up and leave it up. The three methods only disagree about where e comes from: a normal with SD sigma-hat (segment A), the errors this model already made (B), or nothing specified and calibrate instead (E).

Name the promotion out loud - it is the real move and easy to skate past. Until this slide a model was a rule for producing a number; now it is a claim about how the series is made. Every derivation from here is arithmetic on that sentence.

The Gaussian interval is a spread times a multiplier

The Gaussian answer to that question, in one line:

\[e_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \;\text{ i.i.d.}\]

Grant it, and the interval closes into a formula:

\[\hat{y}_{T+1|T} \pm c\,\hat{\sigma} \qquad\text{with}\qquad \hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T - K - M}\sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t^2}\]

  • \(c\) comes off that normal: 1.28 for an 80% band, 1.96 for 95%
  • \(K\) and \(M\) shrink the denominator: parameters estimated, and residuals a model cannot form

What a standard deviation is

Read the first line as the answer to the question left up on the last slide: the error comes from a normal, centred on zero, one spread for every t. Grant that and the formula is arithmetic. Flag that the line is doing more work than it looks - three claims are packed into it and the slide two on takes them out one at a time. Plant it and move.

Two ingredients. The spread is the SD of the one-step residuals; the multiplier comes off the normal above it. The assumption supplies the shape, the residuals supply the size.

K and M are named and not spelled out on purpose - neither appears anywhere else in the course. One sentence each if asked. K is 0 for all four benchmarks, none of which estimates a parameter. M is the residuals a model cannot form: a seasonal naive has no year before its first to copy, so its first 12 months get no fitted value, leaving 405 residuals from 417 training months. Undercounting them - dividing by T instead - makes sigma-hat too small and the band too narrow, which is hard to spot because the numbers still look plausible.

The formula was one step. What about \(h\)?

\(\hat{\sigma}\) measures one-step errors. Put the naive’s forecast into the recipe:

\[y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t, \qquad e_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \;\text{ i.i.d.}\]

Roll it forward. Each step carries one more error:

\[\begin{aligned} y_{T+1} &= y_T + e_{T+1} \\ y_{T+2} &= y_T + e_{T+1} + e_{T+2} \\ y_{T+h} &= y_T + \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{h} e_{T+i} \end{aligned}\]

The forecast is \(y_T\), so the error is that whole sum, and independent errors add variances:

\[\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{h} e_{T+i}\Big) = h\,\sigma^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h}\]

Board pace, not slide pace. The only derivation in the deck, and it pays for the whole next slide - let the room watch each line appear.

Ask the title before you answer it. Sigma-hat was built from one-step residuals, so the last slide’s band is a one-step band, and the chart they have already seen runs out to twenty-four.

The first line is the recipe with one substitution: the naive’s forecast for t is y_{t-1}, so y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t. Nothing new is assumed about the model, only about e. Same e as lecture 1, worth ten seconds: e_t was measured on training data, e_{T+1} is the same object at a time we have not reached. No new symbol.

Roll it forward and let them count. One step, one error; two steps, two. The pattern is visible before the third line lands, so ask for it.

Land the last line deliberately: the VARIANCE is h sigma squared, not the standard deviation. Independent things add variances. Square root at the end and sigma root h falls out - which is why every formula on the next slide has a square root in it.

If asked whether the errors really are independent: they are not, lecture 1 measured exactly that, and the slide after next names all three assumptions. Bank the question and let it arrive.

Each model accumulates errors at its own rate

Run that same count on each model’s own recursion:

Model \(h\)-step forecast standard deviation
Naive \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h}\)
Mean \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1 + 1/T}\)
Seasonal naive \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{k+1}\),   \(k = \lfloor (h-1)/m \rfloor\)
Drift \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h\left(1 + \dfrac{h}{T-1}\right)}\)
  • Seasonal naive: \(y_t = y_{t-m} + e_t\), so reaching \(T+h\) crosses \(k+1\) seasonal cycles, not \(h\) steps
  • Mean and drift carry an extra term because their centre is estimated, not copied

Say this first and the table stops being four things to memorise: every row is the previous slide’s argument run on a different recursion.

The seasonal naive is the same count over a different lag - stepping back by m from T+h lands on observed data after k+1 hops, not h, so k+1 errors accumulate. That is the staircase, and why the band is flat inside a year. Send them back to the fan chart: the step they were told to notice is this k going from 0 to 1.

The mean’s 1 + 1/T is one variance for the noise plus one T-th for not knowing the true mean. Drift’s T-1 is the gaps its slope was averaged over - more history, less added by extrapolating. Both estimate their centre, which the naive and seasonal naive never do, and that is what the extra term buys.

Do not read the formulas out; they are in the cheatsheet. The claim is the title.

The Gaussian interval rests on three assumptions

One line did all of it: \(e_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) i.i.d.

  1. Residuals are uncorrelated: otherwise \(\hat{\sigma}\) understates the spread
  2. Residuals have constant variance: one \(\hat{\sigma}\) for the whole series
  3. Residuals are normally distributed: otherwise \(c = 1.28\) is the wrong multiplier

Lecture 1 failed the first two by looking. The third has never been checked.

Read the three out of the line the sigma-hat slide put up: i.i.d. is independent, so uncorrelated; identically distributed with one sigma is constant variance; the N is normal. The room wrote that line down two slides ago without noticing it was a list of assumptions - which is exactly how it happens in practice.

Count them off on your fingers; the next two segments buy them back one at a time. Two were already measured in lecture 1, and normality is the one left open, which the next slide closes. Bootstrap buys back normality; conformal avoids specifying a shape but substitutes exchangeability. Distribution-free never means assumption-free.

Not normal: the 80% band is too wide

  • Bars above the curve in the middle, still there out in the tails
  • The errors’ own middle 80% (blue) spans 36; \(c = 1.28\) claims 45

The check lecture 1 put off: normality was item 4 and the only one unmarked.

Left panel is constant variance: a rolling SD that climbs while the pooled sigma-hat sits flat through the middle of it. One number standing in for a spread that tripled, too big for the 1980s and too small for the 2010s.

Right panel is a shape comparison, so make it one. The curve is a normal with the SAME SD as these residuals, so shape is the only thing that can differ. Trace where the bars leave it: well above in the middle, still there in the tails after the curve has gone flat. Too peaked and too fat-tailed at once, and no adjustment of the SD fixes that because the SD is already matched.

Then the two pairs of vertical lines, where the abstraction turns into money. Orange is where a normal of this SD puts the central 80%; blue is where these errors actually put it - narrower by about a fifth (36 against 45), which is exactly what segment B’s bootstrap comes back with.

Blue is also off-centre and someone will spot it: the +7.9 bias again. A symmetric plus-or-minus cannot represent that at all.

The fat tails cut the other way further out: at 95% the errors’ own span is about a tenth WIDER, 76 against 69. Sharp peak, fat tails, so the 80% band shrinks and the 95% does not - saying it here makes the bootstrap comparison a confirmation rather than a surprise. All three assumptions now fail; the next two methods each buy one back.

Ch 5.5 · Dropping the normality assumption

Draw the errors this model already made, and simulate forward

Justify with replacement rather than asserting it, and the second reason is the real one. Arithmetic: 5,000 futures of 24 months is 120,000 draws out of 405 numbers, so there is no other option. Honest: we are treating those 405 as a sample of the errors this model makes, not a list of what will happen. Without replacement would be dealing out the past in a new order, a different claim entirely.

Have ready rather than teach: bootstrap_paths shifts the pool to mean zero first, so the +7.9 bias stays in the point forecast instead of sliding the band up with it.

If someone objects that reusing training errors is circular: we reuse their shape, not their order.

Forecast one step, add an error, feed it back

A starred \(y^*\) is invented; a plain \(y\) was observed.

Each trip round the loop is \(y_t = \hat{y}_{t|t-1} + e_t\) again, one step further on:

\[\begin{aligned} y^*_{T+1} &= \hat{y}_{T+1 \,|\, T} + e^*_1 \\ y^*_{T+2} &= \hat{y}_{T+2 \,|\, T+1} + e^*_2 \\ &\;\;\vdots \\ y^*_{T+h} &= \hat{y}_{T+h \,|\, T+h-1} + e^*_h \end{aligned}\]

  • Every line after the first conditions on a \(y^*\) we invented
  • Run the loop out to \(h\) and that is one path

The model enters only through \(\hat{y}\): the loop bootstraps all five.

Draw the loop with your hand as you read the title: forecast, add, feed back, round again. Say the card early - nothing here is specific to the seasonal naive, which is what makes the loop worth learning.

The block is not a new claim: it is the recipe segment A opened with, run forward a step at a time. Every line has the same shape, and it is the shape they already agreed to.

Slow down on the conditioning bars, because that is where the whole segment turns. Line one forecasts from T, the end of the real data. Line two forecasts from T+1 - a value we made up one line ago. The model cannot tell a simulated value from a real one, so it just carries on, and from there the drawn errors compound: a value invented at step 1 is still inside every value after it.

Concrete if the room wants one: the seasonal naive copies last year’s same month, so its first twelve months still reach into real observations and month 13 is the first to copy an invented one. It then carries month 1’s error on top of its own, and month 25 carries three. That is segment A’s k+1 arriving by simulation instead of by derivation - two routes, one arithmetic - and it is why the next slide’s fan steps once a year.

Land the last bullet cleanly and stop: one trip round the loop is one step, the whole loop is ONE path, and one path is not an interval. That is the question the next slide answers.

Repeat 5000 times, then cut each column

The band is not the method. The paths are. Pick one month, collect the 5,000 values the paths have there, sort them, and mark the 0.1 and the 0.9 quantile. Repeat at every month and the marks join up into the band. What a quantile is

Trace one path with your finger from the origin out to 24 months: one possible future, built by the recursion they just watched. Do it on a second and let them see a different future out of the same recipe.

The right panel is the same picture with more paths behind it, and the only new thing is the two orange lines. Stand at one month, look straight down the column of simulated values, sort them, cut where a tenth is left above and a tenth below. Do that at all 24 and you have the two lines.

Say the misconception the way the card does: students meet the bootstrap as a shaded band and assume the band is the method. The order of operations is the other way round.

Two things to point at. The cuts are jagged because the seasonal pattern is inside every path. And they are not equidistant from the middle - a quantile of a lopsided pile comes out lopsided, which plus-or-minus c sigma-hat cannot do at all.

The quantile link draws month 12’s column as its own histogram. Use it if the counting rule is where the room is stuck.

Dropping normality buys a fifth off the 80% band

  • Mean 80% width: 59.7 Gaussian vs 46.4 bootstrap, 22% narrower
  • Mean 95% width: 91.3 vs 90.1, the gap has closed

Same model, same point forecast, same data. Only the assumption changed, and the 80% band got a fifth narrower. That is the sharp peak paying off: the errors’ own middle 80% is tighter than the normal’s, so the empirical quantile beats the formula.

Notice the 95% bands barely differ, while the 80% bands do not. That is the shape of the residuals showing up exactly where you would expect it: the sharp peak makes the central 80% tighter, and the fat tails put the 95% back where the normal had it.

The bootstrap trades normality for i.i.d.

  • Normality is gone. Identically distributed is what bought it
  • One pool, two eras: the residual SD went 8.5 to 26.2
  • Draw from recent errors only and the 80% band widens, 46.4 to 81.2

Wider than the Gaussian’s 59.7 it just beat: the fifth was borrowed from the 1980s.

The previous slide was the win, so open by refusing to take it. Ask where the pool came from: every residual since 1982.

Say why, pointing back at the normality slide’s left panel: the spread tripled, 8.5 to 26.2. A pool mixing the 1980s with the 2010s draws 1980s-sized errors into a 2019 forecast. Identically distributed is the assumption doing the damage, and it is the one the bootstrap spent to buy normality back.

Then the card. Last slide’s headline was a fifth narrower than the Gaussian, 46.4 against 59.7. On an honest pool it is 81.2 - wider than the Gaussian it beat. The win was not a sharper method, it was a stale pool. The Gaussian at least states its constant-variance assumption out loud; the bootstrap hides the same one inside its pool.

C. Conformal prediction

Ch 5.5 · Assuming no distribution at all

Collect the errors you actually made at each horizon, then take their quantile

Roughly 22 minutes into the deck, with about 18 still to run. This is the last segment with new machinery in it, and the densest one. Take a breath first.

The bootstrap’s one pool is the problem

The bootstrap draws from one fixed pool of one-step residuals, shared by every horizon.

  • The errors change over time, so the pool mixes eras: 8.5 then, 26.2 now
  • The win it bought last segment was borrowed from the 1980s

Conformal’s answer: don’t assume one pool. Build a calibration set of \(h\)-step errors, one set per horizon, and assume only exchangeability.

One way to build the set: roll the origin and record the error at each horizon. The next slide is that, on a toy.

Open by refusing the bootstrap’s win, the way segment B’s last slide refused the Gaussian’s. The bootstrap draws from one fixed pool of one-step residuals, and that pool is the assumption doing the damage: it mixes the 1980s and the 2010s in one hat, so a 2019 forecast can draw a 1985-sized error. Last segment’s headline was a fifth narrower than the Gaussian; on an honest pool it was wider than the Gaussian it beat. The win was a stale pool, not a sharper method.

Conformal’s answer is the card: don’t assume one pool. Instead of one pool of one-step errors shared by every horizon, collect the h-step errors this model has already been caught making, one set per horizon, and assume only exchangeability. That is the whole idea; the rest of the segment is the mechanism.

The next slide is one way to build the set: roll the origin and record the error at each horizon. The toy there is the “how” this slide just promised.

A 3-step error needs an origin and a horizon

  1. Stand at \(t = 5\): pretend \(y_6 \dots y_{10}\) are gone, forecast three steps
  2. Subtract what happened: \(e_{8|5} = y_8 - \hat{y}_{8|5}\)
  3. A residual had one origin, yesterday. This error needs two indices
  4. Slide the origin to \(t = 6, 7, \dots\) and collect: the calibration set

Walk this by hand on the numbers. Stand at t=5, the naive forecast for t=8 is whatever y_5 was, then look up what actually happened at t=8 and subtract. That single number is one member of the calibration set.

Slide the origin forward and you get another. That is the whole collection: stand, forecast, subtract, slide. The next slide writes down the quantile of those errors.

Take a quantile of the errors you collected

\[\hat{y}_{T+h|T} \pm Q_{1-\alpha}\big(|e_{t+h|t}|\big)\]

  • \(Q_{1-\alpha}\) is the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of the errors just collected, so 80% takes \(\alpha = 0.2\)
  • One quantile per \(h\), so band growth is measured, not assumed: segment A’s width formulas are not needed
  • No \(\hat{\sigma}\) and no normal table, so this works on a model with no interval formula at all

The price is exchangeability: shuffle the calibration errors and nothing changes. Weaker than the bootstrap’s i.i.d., but a variance that keeps climbing still breaks it.

Exchangeability asks whether the calibration errors and the next error remain comparable when their order is shuffled. It permits dependence that i.i.d. forbids, but does not excuse a changing regime (e.g. non-constant variance over decades).

The interval is an empirical quantile of collected absolute errors: no sigma-hat to estimate, no normal table to look up. Because the width comes out of the data at each h separately, the staircase, the square-root-h, the drift formula: none of segment A’s width machinery is needed. That is also why conformal intervals work on models that have no closed-form formula (ML models, neural nets, Day 3).

Segment B’s pool argument in different clothes: more calibration data is a steadier quantile drawn from a staler regime.

Open by refusing the last slide’s win, the way you refused the bootstrap’s. Where did the eight origins come from, and why eight?

Three lines, same method, same model, same point forecast - only the calibration set changed. Blue is four origins and it is violent: 74.3 on average and one horizon nearly double its neighbour. That is the card, and it is the honest reading of exchangeability. Shuffling the calibration errors has to change nothing. Errors from 1985 and errors from 2018 are not exchangeable on this series, and no amount of distribution-free machinery fixes that, because it was never a distributional assumption.

One implementation note to drop in before the lab: n_windows is a request, not a count. Under 193 points statsforecast silently calibrates on fewer windows than you asked for, and says nothing.

Eight is the course’s choice and it is a judgement, not a default: far enough back for the quantile to mean something, not so far back that it is measuring a different series. Say that out loud - students will otherwise read 8 as a setting that came with the library.

Any model at all can be given a conformal interval

    freq="MS",
)
