A. A forecast is a weighted average

Ch 8.1 · Simple exponential smoothing

Your benchmarks are two ends of one dial

Naiveall the weight on \(y_T\) somewhere in betweenrecent counts more, old still counts Meanequal weight on every \(y_t\)

The naive forecast says the last observation is all that matters. The mean says a point from start of the series matters exactly as much as last point.

This is the slide that makes exponential smoothing feel inevitable rather than invented. Both of yesterday’s simplest benchmarks are weighted averages of the history. They just sit at opposite extremes of the same dial.

Ask the room which one is closer to right for the takeaway food series. Most will say the naive, and they are right, which is exactly why the seasonal naive was yesterday’s best benchmark. But nobody actually believes that only December 2018 carries information about December 2019.

The obvious repair is a weighted average where recent observations count more and the weight fades as you go back. That is the whole idea, and it has a name.

Simple exponential smoothing is the dial between them

\[\hat{y}_{T+1|T} = \alpha y_T + \alpha(1-\alpha) y_{T-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 y_{T-2} + \cdots\]

The weights shrink geometrically as you go back.

\(\alpha\) near 1

Fast to react. Almost the naive forecast.

\(\alpha\) near 0

Slow and smooth. Approaches the mean.

One parameter, alpha, between 0 and 1. It is the smoothing parameter, and it sets how fast the weight decays as you walk backwards through the history.

Point at the two columns and connect them to the previous slide: alpha equal to 1 puts a weight of exactly 1 on the last observation and zero on everything else, which is the naive forecast. Push alpha towards 0 and the weights flatten out towards the mean.

The word “exponential” in the name is the geometric decay, nothing more exotic. If anyone asks why the weights sum to 1, that is the geometric series alpha times the sum of (1 minus alpha) to the j, which is exactly 1.

The same model, written as a state you update

Two lines, and the first one is the whole model:

\[\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1} \qquad\text{(level)}\] \[\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t \qquad\text{(forecast)}\]

\[\begin{aligned} \ell_t &= \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\,\ell_{t-1} \\ &= \alpha y_t + \alpha(1-\alpha)\,y_{t-1} + (1-\alpha)^2\,\ell_{t-2} \\ &= \alpha y_t + \alpha(1-\alpha)\,y_{t-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 y_{t-2} + (1-\alpha)^3\,\ell_{t-3} \end{aligned}\]

\(\ell_t\) is the state: what the model believes the series is worth right now.

\(\alpha\) is fitted

Pick the \(\alpha\) and the starting level \(\ell_0\) that minimize the sum of squared one-step errors on the training data:

\[\text{SSE} = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\right)^2\]

from statsforecast.models import SimpleExponentialSmoothingOptimized
StatsForecast(models=[SimpleExponentialSmoothingOptimized()], freq="MS")

On the spine the optimizer lands on \(\alpha \approx 0\.52\).

SES on its own gives you a flat line

One state, and it is a level. There is nothing in this model that knows the series is growing.

B. Trend: a second state

Ch 8.2 · Holt’s linear trend, and damping it

Holt keeps two numbers

Think of Holt as maintaining two pieces of information:

\[\boxed{\text{What is the series worth right now?}} \;\longrightarrow\; \ell_t\]

\[\boxed{\text{How fast is it changing?}} \;\longrightarrow\; b_t\]

\[\text{future value} \;\approx\; \text{current value} + (\text{rate of change}) \times (\text{time ahead})\]

A second state gives the forecast a slope

\[\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\;\boxed{\,\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}\,} \qquad\text{(level)}\] \[b_t = \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*) b_{t-1} \qquad\text{(slope)}\] \[\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t + h b_t \qquad\text{(forecast)}\]

Put \(h = 1\) in the last line, one step earlier: the boxed term is the forecast Holt already made for \(y_t\).

\[\hat{y}_{t|t-1} = \ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ell_t = \alpha\,y_t + (1-\alpha)\,\hat{y}_{t|t-1}\]

That is the SES level equation unchanged: step from what you predicted towards what you saw. Only the prediction learned a slope.

The slide already shows the h = 1 derivation; the only new thing is the box. The level update now starts from where the trend said it would be, not where it was, and that box is the only difference from SES.

That is word for word the SES level equation, because in SES the one-step forecast was just the level. Nothing about the update changed when the trend arrived; the prediction inside it got better. Say that out loud, because Part C adds seasonality and the level equation will survive that too.

Beta-star is a second smoothing parameter with the same meaning as alpha: how fast does the model revise its belief about the slope when the data disagrees. It is fitted alongside alpha and the two initial states.

The pattern is the thing to notice. Every state in this family is updated by the same move: a weighted step from what you believed towards what you just saw.

Holt adds a slope but not a December

Better, and still wrong in an obvious way.

The line now rises, which is progress you can see. Over 24 months it climbs from about 356 to about 373, which is roughly the right slope for this series.

And it is still a straight line through a series that visibly swings by 30 percent between February and December. The residuals from this model would fail every diagnostic in yesterday’s lecture, for a reason the picture already gives away.

Two states means two things remembered. Nothing here knows where it is in the cycle.

Extrapolate a straight line far enough and it lies

At \(h = 60\) the two disagree by about 38 units, on a series whose last observation was 393.

Empirically, forecasts that extrapolate a constant slope forever are too optimistic at long horizons. Hyndman’s damped trend is the standard repair and it is one extra parameter.

The damping parameter phi sits between 0 and 1 and multiplies the slope at every step out, so the contributions form a geometric series that converges. The forecast approaches a horizontal asymptote instead of running off the top of the chart.

Point at the gap at the right edge. Five years out, the undamped line has added 38 units that the damped one has not. Neither is verified here, and that is the point: nothing in the training data can tell you which is right five years out, so the conservative one is the better default.

Damping turns the slope sum into a geometric series

\[\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t + h\,b_t \qquad\text{(undamped)}\] \[\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t + (\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots + \phi^h)\,b_t \qquad\text{(damped)}\]

With \(\phi < 1\) that sum converges, so the forecast has a ceiling:

\[\hat{y}_{t+h|t} \;\longrightarrow\; \ell_t + \frac{\phi}{1-\phi}\,b_t\]

Same move as the SES slide, pointed the other way. There they unrolled the recursion backwards over the history and the weights fell out. Here you unroll the forecast forwards over the horizon, and what falls out is the flattening they just watched.

The slide does the unrolling; the point is that a geometric series with ratio below 1 has a finite sum, so the forecast has a ceiling.

Write the ceiling on the board if it helps: phi over one minus phi. It is worth computing one value live, because the numbers surprise people. At 0.9 the model can only ever add nine slopes to the current level, no matter how far out you ask. That is a hard cap on the forecast, chosen by one parameter.

Then set up the next slide’s bullet about 0.8. Phi of 0.8 caps the whole forecast at four slopes, which on monthly data is gone inside half a year. That is why fitted phi almost always lands high, between 0.8 and 0.98, and why anything much lower means the trend has effectively been switched off.

C. Seasonality: the third state

Ch 8.3 · Holt-Winters

The third state updates like the other two

The forecast gains one term: the matching value from the last cycle.

\[\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t + h\,b_t + \underbrace{s_{t+h-m(k+1)}}_{\text{the matching season value}}\]

From Day 2 take the trend out, keep what is left:

\[y_t - \underbrace{(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})}_{\text{trend}} \;=\; \text{the season this period}\]

\[s_t = \gamma\,(y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma)\,s_{t-m} \qquad\text{(season)}\]

Read it in the order it is built. The forecast needs a season value, so the state has to hold one per position in the cycle: twelve of them for monthly data, each refreshed once per cycle rather than once per step.

The middle line is Day 2’s decomposition, written as one line. There they subtracted a fitted trend and called the remainder seasonal; here the level and slope are the trend, and the remainder is the raw season value for this period.

The last line is the same weighted step they have now seen three times, and the level and slope equations do not change at all. They work on the observation with its season removed, y minus s at t minus m, exactly as in Part B.

The k in the forecast subscript just counts complete cycles; do not spend more than a sentence on it.

Gamma is worth one question out loud: how much should one unusual year change what the model believes about that point in the cycle?

Additive or multiplicative is a real choice

Additive season

\(y_t = \ell_t + s_t\)

A season adds the same number of units, whatever the level is.

Multiplicative season

\(y_t = \ell_t \times s_t\)

A season is a fixed percentage of the level.

What the spine does

Its seasonal swing grew from a few units in the 1980s to about 55 by 2018, right alongside the level.

This is the choice Day 1 already made them look at, in different clothes. When they log-transformed this series in lecture 2, the reason was that the seasonal swings grow with the level. Same observation, now a model decision.

The numbers in the callout are read off the fitted seasonal state, which is on screen a few slides from now, so this is a promise the deck keeps.

There are two ways out and the course teaches both: model the season as multiplicative, or transform the data until the season is additive. Part D does that pair properly.

Holt-Winters has the right shape

Flat, then tilted, then the right shape. One state added per slide.

Compare the Holt-Winters line against the dashed holdout. It puts the peaks and the troughs in the right places and at roughly the right size. It is not perfect and it does not need to be.

If someone asks whether this beats the seasonal naive: good question, hold it for Part E, because answering it properly needs eight folds and not this chart.

D. The ETS family

Ch 8.4 to 8.6 · The taxonomy, and the search over it

Three letters name the model

Error Trend Season
N = none no trend no season
A = additive additive linear trend additive
Ad = damped damped trend
M = multiplicative multiplicative multiplicative

ETS(A,N,N) is SES. ETS(A,A,N) is Holt. ETS(A,A,A) is additive Holt-Winters. Parts A, B and C, renamed.

Two errors, five trends, three seasons: about thirty models, and you have already built three of them.

Do not teach this table row by row. Put it up, say what the three letters are, and spend the time on the line underneath, which is the payoff.

The one thing worth saying about the error letter, because it is the least intuitive: an additive error is a fixed number of units, a multiplicative error is a fixed percentage. On a series that grew by a factor of eight, that distinction is not cosmetic.

A handful of the thirty are unstable and get excluded, which is why the card says “about”.

The error term makes it a model

Holt-Winters gives one number. ETS also says where the randomness enters.

\[y_t = \underbrace{\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + s_{t-m}}_{\text{what the states predict}} \;+\; \underbrace{\varepsilon_t}_{\text{the error}}\]

Now the model can say how likely the data it saw was. That number is its likelihood, and it is what lets you rank models.

It can also draw many futures instead of one line. The spread of those futures is the interval.

This is the answer to “why not just use Holt-Winters and stop”. The point forecasts are identical. What the state space form adds is the error term.

Connect it to yesterday explicitly. To get an interval out of a seasonal naive they had to supply an assumption: Gaussian errors, bootstrapped residuals, or conformal calibration. ETS carries its own error distribution instead, so the interval is part of the model, and it emits quantiles directly.

That is exactly why Part E can score it on scaled CRPS without a line of new plumbing.

AICc picks one member of the family

\[\text{AICc} = \underbrace{-2\log L}_{\text{how badly it fits}} \;+\; \underbrace{2k}_{\text{a toll per parameter}} \;+\; \text{a small-sample correction}\]

A bigger model always fits better, so fit alone would always pick the biggest one. The toll makes it pay for every parameter it asks for.

Lower is better, only the gaps between models mean anything, and AutoETS keeps the smallest.

Read the formula left to right in two beats. Minus two log-likelihood is the misfit and it falls as the model explains more. Two k is the toll: every parameter costs two points, whether or not it earned them.

The correction is what the extra c stands for, it matters at small sample sizes, and at 441 observations it is a rounding error. Do not spend time on it.

The caveat worth stating: the number only compares models fit to the same data. It says nothing about whether any of them is good, only which is best, which is why Part E still scores the winner out of sample.

The season changes the score. The trend does not

Drop the season and the score jumps by hundreds. Add a trend and it hardly moves.

Every bar here is a real fit on the spine, and the axis is the distance from the winner rather than the raw AICc, because the raw numbers are all near 4500 and only the differences matter.

Read the shape, not the values. The two models with no seasonal letter sit about 475 points out. Among the four seasonal ones, adding a trend moves the score by 16 points one way and 7 the other.

Same conclusion Day 1 reached from the ACF: on retail data, seasonality is what discriminates.

What it picked has no trend term

selected      : ETS(M,N,A)
alpha (level) : 0.525
gamma (season): 0.321

Multiplicative error, no trend state, additive season. On a series that grew by a factor of eight.

This is the slide that earns the segment, so give it room and let the room react before explaining it.

The explanation is in the level equation from Part A. With alpha at 0.52, the level takes a half-step towards every new observation, so it climbs perfectly well on its own. A trend state would let the model extrapolate that climb, and that is the part AICc rejected: two more parameters, and a slope that has to be right twelve months out.

Connect it to Part B’s callout. This is what “damping loses on this series” looked like from the other end: the search did not merely damp the trend, it removed it.

The lesson worth naming: an automatic search optimizes the criterion you gave it, not the story you had in your head.

The states it learned are a decomposition

The level climbed without a trend state, and the season grew from a few units in the 1980s to about 55 by 2018.

Deliberately the same picture Day 1 drew with STL, in the same panel vocabulary, because these are the same kind of object.

The season panel pays the promise made three slides back: a few units in the 1980s, plus 55 by 2018. That is multiplicative behaviour showing up inside an additive seasonal state, and the model copes with it through the multiplicative error term, which is the next slide’s subject.

If anyone asks why the season panel is noisier late in the series: the swings are bigger in absolute terms, so a fixed percentage of noise is a bigger number.

Two ways to handle a season that grows

Change the data

Transform until the swings hold still, forecast, then transform back. Day 1 used the log.

Change the model

A multiplicative error already works in percentages. This is what AutoETS chose here.

\[w_t = \begin{cases} \log(y_t) & \lambda = 0 \\ (y_t^{\lambda}-1)/\lambda & \lambda \neq 0\end{cases}\]

Box-Cox is one dial across the transforms: \(\lambda = 1\) is none, \(\lambda = 0\) is the log.

The two columns are the honest summary of the choice. Both routes handle variance that grows with the level. Transforming is more explicit and leaves you owning the back-transformation. Letting the error be multiplicative is one letter in the model name.

Do not present either as correct. They are the same idea entering the model at two different points, and this course took the second route by accident, because it let the search choose.

Five lines, and four of them are yesterday’s. Walk the code: the cross-validation call is the one they wrote in Exercise 2.5, with the same h, the same step size, the same eight windows, and score_cv is the scoring they did by hand there, packaged.

That is what a harness is for, and it is worth naming, because this afternoon a framework will do the same job with a different API and they should be able to see what it is buying.

Say what happens if a model has no closed-form interval, since they will hit it in the capstone: cross-validation refuses to score levels and asks for prediction intervals. Conformal, from yesterday’s segment E. ETS does not need it.

ETS wins on one window and ties over eight folds

One window: 0.97 against 1.11. Eight folds: 1.176 against 1.183, inside a fold spread of 0.72 to 2.02.

This is Exercise 2.4 and 2.5 happening again, to a different model, and it is worth saying so out loud before explaining the chart.

Left panel: on the single 24-month window ETS improves on the seasonal naive by 13 percent, the kind of number that gets a model shipped. Over eight folds both land on 1.18 and the win evaporates. Yesterday the decomposition route did exactly this, in exactly this direction, on this same series.

Right panel is why. The fold-to-fold spread runs from about 0.72 to about 2.02 for both models, so a 0.007 difference in the means is a small fraction of the difference between a good year and a bad one.

If someone asks whether the win is real: eight folds cannot settle a gap this size, and the next move is more folds or more series, not a louder claim. That is why score_cv returns mase_min and mase_max.

Its 80% band covers 87.5%

More coverage than promised is not better. It means the band is too wide.

Both bars miss, in opposite directions, and the ETS miss is the interesting one because it looks like good news. The chart calls a band honest within five points of nominal, which the seasonal naive clears and ETS does not.

Ask the room which model they would rather ship, before explaining. Most rooms pick the 87.5 percent, because more coverage sounds safer.

A band that claims 80 and delivers 87.5 is wrong by 7.5 points, and it is wrong in the direction that costs money: every decision sized off it is hedged more than the evidence requires.

Recall yesterday’s rule while it is on screen: coverage checks a band, it cannot rank two bands. To rank them you need a proper score.

The wider band scores worse

ETS has the wider band on the left and the worse CRPS on the right.

This is exactly yesterday’s STL comparison run in reverse, and it is worth saying so. Yesterday the decomposition route had the narrowest bands and the worst coverage and still lost on CRPS. Today ETS has the widest bands and the most coverage and still loses on CRPS.

Neither sharpness nor coverage ranks a forecast on its own. CRPS does, because it scores the whole distribution against what actually happened.

The seasonal naive is not on top here because it is clever. It is on top because its band is roughly the right size and roughly in the right place.

What to report

A fitted, selected model matched a one-line benchmark on the point forecast and did worse on the interval. Report that.

This is where a lot of forecasting projects quietly go wrong, so make the point directly. A team that only tracked MASE would ship ETS today and write in the summary that the new model beat the benchmark.

They would be reporting a 0.6 percent point-forecast improvement while shipping intervals seven points too wide, and every downstream decision sized off those intervals would be more conservative than the data justifies.

The floor holding is not a failure of the day. It is the strongest evidence this course can offer that benchmarks are not a formality. A seasonal naive took one line, took no fitting time, and is still the best-calibrated model in the room.
