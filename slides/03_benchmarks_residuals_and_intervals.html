A. The benchmark floor

Ch 5.1–5.2 · Simple, strong baselines

Four benchmarks set the floor a model must clear

  • Mean: average of all historical observations
  • Naive: last observed value (\(\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_T\))
  • Seasonal Naive: same season from previous year (\(\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_{T+h-m}\))
  • Drift: last value plus average historical trend slope

Always fit these 4 first. On many series, Seasonal Naive is tough to beat.

Do not let these sound like toys. On a seasonal series a seasonal naive is a genuinely hard opponent, and by the end of today they will have watched it beat a decomposition model over eight folds.

Worth saying which is which in plain language: the mean forgets time entirely, the naive remembers only yesterday, the seasonal naive remembers this month last year, and the drift draws a straight line through the first and last points. Ask the room which one they expect to win here before you show them.

A benchmark is what turns a score into evidence

  • Objective evidence: proves the complex model adds value
  • Sanity check: catches complex ML models losing to naive rules
  • Scale baseline: provides a standard denominator for errors (MASE)
  • Fast: takes just a few lines of code

The question to ask in every review

“What is the simple baseline, and by how much did we beat it?”

This is the slide to slow down on if the room is senior. The question in the callout is the one to take back to work: it costs nothing to ask, and it is the single most effective check on a forecasting claim.

The MASE point is worth a sentence now because the metric arrives in lecture 2: the benchmark is not only a rival, it is also the denominator that makes errors comparable across series.

B. Residuals

Ch 5.3–5.4 · Has the model finished?

Residuals flatter, forecast errors do not

Residual (\(e_t\)): Error on in-sample training data (one-step ahead)

\[e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\]

Two errors to keep straight

  • Residuals = In-sample training errors (flattering)
  • Forecast Errors = Out-of-sample holdout errors (honest)

This distinction causes more confusion later than anything else in the day, so make it concrete: a residual is what the model got wrong on data it had already seen; a forecast error is what it got wrong on data it had not. Residuals flatter, forecast errors do not.

Everything in segment B is residuals. Everything in lecture 2 is forecast errors. Say that now and it saves a question later.

Two failures mean fix the model, two mean fix the band

Essential: fix the model

  1. Uncorrelated (\(r_k \approx 0\))
    \(\rightarrow\) signal still on the table
  2. Zero mean (\(\bar{e} = 0\))
    \(\rightarrow\) forecast runs high or low

Useful: fix the interval

  1. Constant variance
    \(\rightarrow\) one width for all eras
  2. Normally distributed
    \(\rightarrow\) \(1.28\) is the wrong step

A model can pass the left column and fail the right. The point forecast is fine; the band around it is not.

Say the split as an instruction rather than as a taxonomy. A left-column failure is a message about the model: go back and change it. A right-column failure leaves the point forecast alone and tells you the band around it is wrong.

Pause on the dot before the bottom card and ask the room which column they expect the seasonal naive to fail. The next slide answers it.

One honest caveat if someone asks: uncorrelated appears on both sides. It is essential for the model, and it is also one of the three assumptions behind the Gaussian interval, which segment C spells out.

Segments C, D and E are, in order, the three ways to stop relying on the right column.

The best benchmark here fails three of the four

P.residual_diagnostics(resid, ds=fv["ds"])

  • Constant variance fails: SD \(8.5\) in the first decade, \(26.2\) in the last
  • Uncorrelated fails: ACF spikes stay outside the bounds

Three panels, and the three bullets read straight off them.

The time plot sits above zero far more than below, which is the bias: a seasonal naive repeats last year on a series that grows about 6% a year, so it runs low every month. Mean residual +7.9 against a series in the hundreds.

The same panel fans out, so constant variance fails. The ACF spikes well outside the bounds, so uncorrelated fails.

The fourth property, normality, is not on this slide because it is segment C’s business. The histogram is already telling you the answer if you look at it.

Do not treat any of this as a failure of the seasonal naive. It is the honest state of the best benchmark on this series, and every interval method later in this deck is a response to one of these lines.

Ljung-Box tests all the lags at once

\[Q = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{r_k^2}{T-k}\]

  • \(p < 0.05\): autocorrelation is real, so the model is not finished
  • \(p \ge 0.05\): residuals are indistinguishable from white noise
r = resid.dropna()
p = acorr_ljungbox(r, lags=[24], return_df=True)["lb_pvalue"].iloc[0]
print(f"{len(r)} residuals, lag 24:  p = {p:.1e}")
405 residuals, lag 24:  p = 8.6e-241

Rule of thumb: use lag l=10 for non-seasonal data, l=2m for seasonal data.

C. Prediction intervals

Ch 5.5 · Quantifying uncertainty

A range is what a decision can act on

Point forecast

“Demand will be 340 units.”

  • Guaranteed to be wrong
  • Hides the operational risk

Interval forecast

“Demand: [300, 380], 80% of the time.”

  • Sizes the safety stock
  • States the risk out loud

Decisions like staffing buffers, warehouse capacity, and safety inventory depend directly on the interval width.

The future is a range, not a point

At forecast origin \(T\), \(Y_{T+h}\) is still unknown:

\[Y_{T+h}\mid \mathcal{I}_T\]

  • Forecast distribution: plausible future values, given what is known at \(T\)
  • Point forecast: one centre, usually a mean or median
  • 80% interval: 10th to 90th percentile
  • 95% interval: 2.5th to 97.5th percentile

The unknown future value, not the prediction, is represented by a distribution.

Start with what is uncertain. We produce one point forecast, but we do not believe the future must equal that point. Before it is observed, we represent the future as plausible outcomes conditional on what we know now.

The point forecast is one summary. An interval takes two quantiles. The rest of the deck asks how to construct them: impose a Gaussian shape, simulate futures from empirical errors, or calibrate a set from past forecast errors.

Gaussian intervals impose a shape

Estimate the residual spread:

\[\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T - K - M}\sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t^2}\]

Then step out from the point forecast:

\[\hat{y}_{T+1|T} \pm c\,\hat{\sigma}\]

  • \(K\) = estimated parameters; \(M\) = missing residuals
  • \(c = 1.28\) for 80%; \(c = 1.96\) for 95%

Two things students trip on. The M term: a seasonal naive cannot produce a fitted value for its first twelve months, so twelve residuals are missing and the denominator has to know that. And c is a normal quantile - 1.28 and 1.96 are only right if the residuals are normal, which we just showed they are not.

Flag that now. The rest of the deck is the repair.

How fast the range widens depends on the method

Benchmark \(h\)-step forecast standard deviation
Mean \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1 + 1/T}\)
Naive \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h}\)
Seasonal naive \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{k+1}\),   \(k = \lfloor (h-1)/m \rfloor\)
Drift \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h\left(1 + \dfrac{h}{T-1}\right)}\)

\(\sqrt{h}\) is the naive case, not a law

For a seasonal naive the width is a staircase: flat inside a year, one step up per complete year crossed. \(k\) counts those years.

All four formulas assume the residuals are uncorrelated. That assumption is already known to be false here - segment B just rejected it at p ~ 1e-240.

The interval is the forecast, the line is its middle

Make the room read the dashed actual line against the bands rather than admiring the shading. Ask how many of the 24 points fall inside the 80% band. It looks excellent here, and lecture 2 shows that a single 24-point window cannot measure a coverage rate at all.

If you want one sentence for this slide: the interval is the forecast, and the line down the middle is just its most likely value.

The Gaussian interval rests on three assumptions

  1. Residuals are uncorrelated: otherwise \(\hat{\sigma}\) understates the spread
  2. Residuals have constant variance: one \(\hat{\sigma}\) for the whole series
  3. Residuals are normally distributed: otherwise \(c = 1.28\) is the wrong multiplier

On this series all three fail. The next two methods each buy one of them back.

Count them off on your fingers, because the next three segments buy them back one at a time and it helps if the room is counting with you. Bootstrap buys back normality. Conformal avoids specifying a distributional shape, but replaces those assumptions with exchangeability. Distribution-free never means assumption-free.

D. Bootstrapped intervals

Ch 5.5 · Dropping the normality assumption

Roughly 55 minutes in, and the real break is still 40 minutes away at the deck boundary. Take two minutes here and let the room stand up. Segments D and E are the densest stretch of Day 2, and they land better on a room that has moved.

Resample the errors you actually saw

A one-step error is \(e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\). For the naive method \(\hat{y}_{t|t-1} = y_{t-1}\), so \(y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t\), and a future value can be simulated by drawing a past error:

\[y^*_{T+1} = y_T + e^*_{T+1}, \qquad y^*_{T+2} = y^*_{T+1} + e^*_{T+2}, \qquad \dots\]

For the seasonal naive the same recursion runs on the seasonal lag:

\[y^*_{T+i} = y^*_{T+i-m} + e^*_{T+i}\]

The star means “one possible future that could have occurred”, not the observed value. Run it 5000 times and you have 5000 possible futures.

The assumption you just made

The simple residual bootstrap assumes the residuals come from one common distribution \(\hat{F}\) whose distributional characteristics do not change over time: i.i.d. draws from the pool of past errors.

  • Bought: normality. The interval is now whatever shape the errors are, and need not be symmetric.
  • Still owed: independence, and identical distribution.
  • On this series the second one is false: the residual SD tripled.

Every interval method spends an assumption; the honest question is which one. The bootstrap trades normality for identical distribution, and on this series that trade is a bad one - the residual SD tripled, so 1980s errors are in the same draw pool as 2010s errors.

That is not a reason to avoid the bootstrap. It is a reason to know which knob you are turning, and the lab turns it: restricting the pool to recent residuals moves coverage from about 61% to about 87%.

5000 possible futures

P.sim_paths_plot(train, fc["ds"], boot_paths, n_show=8, actual=test)

The band is not the method. The paths are the method; the band is a percentile taken down each column.

This is the slide that fixes the most common misconception about the bootstrap. Students see the shaded band and assume the band is the method. It is not. The method is these paths; the band is a percentile taken down each column afterwards.

Trace one path with your finger from the origin out to 24 months, and say: this is one possible future, drawn by taking last year’s value and adding a real error we actually observed at some point in the past.

E. Conformal prediction

Ch 5.5 · Distribution-free intervals, and the only method that works on a model with no formula

Name the error first: \(e_{t+h|t}\)

\[e_{t+h|t} = y_{t+h} - \hat{y}_{t+h|t}\]

Symbol Meaning
\(t\) the time the forecast was made: the forecast origin
\(h\) the forecast horizon
\(t+h\) the future time being predicted
\(y_{t+h}\) what actually happened
\(\hat{y}_{t+h\,\vert\,t}\) the forecast made at time \(t\) for time \(t+h\)

Note

At \(h = 1\) these are exactly the residuals from part B. For \(h > 1\) they are a different, wider set of errors, and they must be collected, not fitted.

Notation discipline pays off here. e_{t+h|t} carries two indices for a reason: which point is being forecast, and which origin it was forecast from. A 12-step error made in 2010 and a 12-step error made in 2016 are two members of the same calibration set.

Residuals had one index because there was only ever one origin: yesterday.

A worked example of \(e_{t+h|t}\)

  • Walk-through
  • Plot

You hold \(y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{10}\) and want a 3-step-ahead interval.

  1. Stand at \(t = 5\). Pretend \(y_6 \dots y_{10}\) do not exist yet.
  2. Forecast three steps out: \(\hat{y}_{8|5}\).
  3. Now look up what really happened: \(y_8\).
  4. Record \(e_{{8|5}} = y_8 - \hat{{y}}_{{8|5}}\), one 3-step-ahead error.

. . .

Slide the origin to \(t = 6, 7, \dots\) and repeat. What you collect is the calibration set for \(h = 3\).

Walk this by hand on the numbers. Stand at t=5, the naive forecast for t=8 is whatever y_5 was, then look up what actually happened at t=8 and subtract. That single number is one member of the calibration set.

Slide the origin forward and you get another. Conformal prediction is nothing more elaborate than collecting those and taking a quantile of their absolute values.

Distribution-free is not assumption-free

Coverage requires the calibration errors and the next error to be exchangeable.

Shuffle their order: none should look as if it came from a different regime.

Use an empirical quantile of the collected errors:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h|T} \pm Q_{1-\alpha}(|e_{t+h|t}|)\]

  • One quantile per horizon \(h\): widening with \(h\) is measured, not assumed
  • No \(\hat{\sigma}\), no \(c\), no parametric shape: hence distribution-free

This is the slide that earns the segment, so do not rush it.

I.i.d. says every error is an independent draw from one fixed distribution. Exchangeability asks whether the calibration errors and the next error remain comparable when their order is shuffled. It can permit dependence that i.i.d. forbids, but it does not excuse a changing regime. If recent errors are systematically larger than old errors, their order carries information and exchangeability fails too.

Then the payoff. Because the interval is an empirical quantile of errors the model actually made, there is no sigma-hat to estimate and no c to look up, and nothing assumes a parametric shape. This constructs a calibrated prediction interval; it does not necessarily estimate a complete forecast distribution. That is why it works on a model whose interval has no formula, which is most of what Day 3 covers.

statsforecast does exactly this internally: n_windows rolling origins, the absolute error at each horizon, then the empirical quantile of those.

Any model at all can be given a conformal interval

from statsforecast.utils import ConformalIntervals

sf_conf = StatsForecast(
    models=[SeasonalNaive(
        season_length=12,
        prediction_intervals=ConformalIntervals(n_windows=8, h=24),
    )],
    freq="MS",
)
fc_conf = sf_conf.forecast(df=train, h=24, level=[80, 95])
  • n_windows: how many rolling origins make up the calibration set
  • Needs at least \(2h + 1\) observations; more windows, steadier quantiles
  • With 8 windows only 16 scores sit behind each horizon, so the band is visibly jittery

This is the practical payoff of the whole segment and the thing a Day 3 author will need: any model that has no closed-form interval gets one from prediction_intervals=ConformalIntervals(…). Not every statsforecast model can produce intervals on its own; all of them can produce conformal ones.

The jitter warning is worth making concrete. With eight windows there are only sixteen scores behind each horizon, so the band wobbles. That is a small sample showing through, not a bug.

The printed numbers are the trap, so read them out and then say plainly where they came from: 24 points. The next slide is the arithmetic on why that cannot separate three methods.

Every interval method spends an assumption

Method Assumes Gives
Gaussian uncorrelated · constant variance · normal closed form
Bootstrap uncorrelated · i.i.d. from \(\hat{F}\) any error shape
Conformal exchangeable \(h\)-step errors no parametric shape

Important

Pick by which assumption you can defend, not by the widths on the last slide.

The table is the takeaway. Each row is a trade, and the honest question when choosing is not “which is most accurate” but “which of these three assumptions am I willing to make about this series”.

If the room wants a default: conformal, because it assumes least, and because it is the one that generalises to models that have no formula. That is also why it is the one Day 3 will lean on. But say the qualification out loud: its interval is distribution-free, not assumption-free, and a changing error regime can break exchangeability.

Why one test window is misleading

On a single small test set (\(n = 24\)), coverage error is large:

\[\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.8 \times 0.2}{24}} \approx \pm 8\% \quad (\pm 16\% \text{ at } 95\% \text{ CI})\]

A nominal 80% interval could easily measure anywhere from 64% to 96% on a single 24-point holdout by chance alone.

Over 8 rolling origins (96 scored points) they separate cleanly:

80% coverage over 96 scored points:   Gaussian 77%   bootstrap 61%   conformal 83%

The bootstrap’s 61% is the identically-distributed assumption being punished: pooling 1980s residuals (SD 8.5) with 2010s residuals (SD 26) makes the draw pool far too tight for a forecast issued in 2016. Restricting the pool to the last 120 residuals takes it to about 87%; the last 60 push it to 91%. The assumption is the knob, and the lab turns it.
