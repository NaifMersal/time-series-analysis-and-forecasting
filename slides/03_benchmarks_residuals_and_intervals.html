A. The benchmark floor

Ch 5.1–5.2 · Simple, strong baselines

Four benchmarks set the floor a model must clear

Throughout: \(T\) is the last observed time, the forecast origin; \(h\) is how many steps ahead; \(m\) is the seasonal period, 12 for monthly data.

  • Mean: \(\hat{y}_{T+h} = \bar{y}\), the average of all \(T\) observations
  • Naive: \(\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_T\), the last observed value
  • Seasonal naive: \(\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_{T+h-m(k+1)}\), the same month a whole number of years back, where \(k = \lfloor (h-1)/m \rfloor\) counts the complete years the horizon crosses
  • Drift: \(\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_T + \dfrac{h}{T-1}(y_T - y_1)\), the line drawn through the first and the last observation, extended

Always fit these 4 first. On many series, seasonal naive is tough to beat.

Do not let these sound like toys. On a seasonal series a seasonal naive is a genuinely hard opponent, and by the end of today they will have watched it beat a decomposition model over eight folds.

Worth saying which is which in plain language: the mean forgets time entirely, the naive remembers only yesterday, the seasonal naive remembers this month last year, and the drift draws a straight line through the first and last points. Ask the room which one they expect to win here before you show them.

Two bits of the algebra pay off later, so read them out rather than skipping. The drift denominator is T-1 because T observations have T-1 gaps between them, which is why its interval width carries a T-1 as well. And k is 0 whenever the horizon stays inside one year, so for h up to 12 the seasonal naive is just “last year’s same month”; k only starts counting at h = 13, and that is exactly where its prediction interval takes its first step up. Segment C uses the same k for the same reason.

A benchmark is what turns a score into evidence

  • Objective evidence: proves the complex model adds value
  • Sanity check: catches complex ML models losing to naive rules
  • Scale baseline: provides a standard denominator for errors (MASE)
  • Fast: takes just a few lines of code

The question to ask in every review

“What is the simple baseline, and by how much did we beat it?”

This is the slide to slow down on if the room is senior. The question in the callout is the one to take back to work: it costs nothing to ask, and it is the single most effective check on a forecasting claim.

The MASE point is worth a sentence now because the metric is defined properly in lecture 2: the benchmark is not only a rival, it is also the denominator that makes errors comparable across series. Two slides on, a chart legend quotes MASE numbers, and the line under it gives the room just enough to read them.

Decomposition is also a forecasting method

Ch 5.7 · You already built every step on Day 1

Series\(y_t\) STLsplits off the season Forecastdrift · seasonal naive Recombineone forecast

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = \hat{A}_{T+h} + \hat{S}_{T+h}\]

MSTL(season_length=12, trend_forecaster=RandomWalkWithDrift()) runs this entire pipeline in one call.

Ch 5.7. Every step is something they built yesterday: STL, the seasonally adjusted series A_t (trend + remainder), and the seasonal component S_t. The two pieces are forecast separately (drift on A_t, seasonal naive on S_t) and added back.

The course pins trend_forecaster to drift on purpose: the default is AutoETS (Day 3), and we do not want a Day 3 model quietly winning a Day 2 comparison.

The plus sign assumes additive seasonality (swings stay constant in size). Multiplicative (swings grow with level) is handled by logging first.

B. Residuals

Ch 5.3–5.4 · Has the model finished?

Residuals flatter, forecast errors do not

Residual (\(e_t\)): one-step error on training data \[e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\]

Residuals (\(e_t\))

  • In-sample training errors

  • Flattering to the model

Forecast Errors (\(e_{T+h}\))

  • Out-of-sample holdout errors

  • Honest evidence

Seasonal naive: \(e_t = y_t - y_{t-12}\) (the raw year-on-year change).

The holdout has not happened yet. The only evidence a model has already given you is what it got wrong on the training data.

Read yhat_{t|t-1} as “the forecast of y_t from data up to t-1” (the bar means given).

Residuals flatter, forecast errors do not. Everything in segment B is residuals. Everything in lecture 2 is forecast errors.

For a seasonal naive, the residual is simple subtraction: this March minus last March. Every number in the rest of the segment is a fact about that subtraction.

Start by looking at what the winner left behind

P.residual_time_plot(resid, ds=fv["ds"], show_mean=True, era_months=120,
                     era_labels=("first 10 years", "last 10 years"),

                     title="Seasonal naive: 405 one-step residuals")

  • It fans out: the typical miss grew from 8.5 to 26.2, 3x as wide

Put the figure up and say nothing for a moment. Ask the room what is wrong with it before you reveal either bullet. Both faults are visible without a single statistic, which is the whole reason this panel gets a slide to itself.

The first answer you will get is that it is mostly above the zero line. Good: that is bias, and the dashed line is the mean. The second, which usually needs a prompt, is that the left third of the picture is thin and the right third is tall. That is the variance changing, and the two shaded stretches are the same observation written as a number.

Say what those two SDs are, because “SD” on its own is a statistic and this is a quantity: the shaded spans are the first ten years of residuals and the last ten, and each SD is how far a typical month missed by inside its own span, in the millions of dollars the y axis is drawn in. Early on the model was usually out by about 8; by the 2010s, by about 26. The series it is forecasting grew over the same period, which is why the errors grew with it - the misses did not get worse in relative terms, they got bigger in absolute ones.

Do not name the four properties yet. Two of them just walked out of the picture; the naming slide comes after the room has seen why they exist.

The bias is the trend, arriving as an error

\(e_t = y_t - y_{t-12}\), so the mean residual is the average year-on-year gain:

  • The spine gains about 5% of its average level each year, and +7.9 is that gain arriving one month at a time
  • A seasonal naive repeats last year. It has no term for growth, so it hands the growth back as error
  • This is not a bad fit. It is structure the model never had

Which is exactly why the STL + drift route beat it in segment A: drift is the missing term.

This is the slide that turns a diagnostic number into a diagnosis. Students can read “+7.9” off any residual summary; what they cannot usually do is say where it came from. Here they can, because the residual is a subtraction they understand.

Work the arithmetic out loud. Turnover grows about 5% a year on a series averaging 160, so a typical year adds roughly 8. The seasonal naive predicts this March from last March, so it is short by one year of growth every single month, and the average of those shortfalls is the mean residual. The two numbers are the same fact.

Then land the card. Segment A showed the STL route winning the window and did not say why. This is why. That connection is worth pausing on: the residual check did not merely flag a problem, it named the fix, and the fix was already on the leaderboard.

If someone asks why we do not just subtract 7.9 from every forecast: you can, and that is a bias correction. It fixes the average and leaves the growth unmodelled, so it is wrong again next year at a different size. Better to give the model a trend.

Send them back to Day 1’s table of five shapes before you interpret anything. That table was built to read a series; it reads residuals with no change at all, because residuals are just another series. Ask what shape is on the screen and wait for someone to say slow decay.

Then make both halves of the reading count, because the orange lags are the good news and students skate past them. Nothing at 12 or 24 means the seasonal naive did the job it was named for: it took the season out completely. What it left is the part it was never built to take, and that part has the trend signature.

So the two slides agree. The mean residual said the model is short by a year of growth; the ACF says consecutive errors are short by nearly the same amount, which is what a slowly moving level looks like. One fault, two instruments.

If asked why r_1 is so close to 1 rather than small: neighbouring months share almost all of their shortfall, because the level moves slowly. It decays to nothing by lag 11 because eleven months is long enough for the level to have moved on.

Ljung-Box tests all the lags at once

Joint hypothesis test on \(\ell\) residual autocorrelations:

\[Q = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{r_k^2}{T-k} \qquad Q \sim \chi^2_{\ell-K} \;\text{ under } H_0\]

  • \(p < 0.05\): Autocorrelation is real \(\rightarrow\) model is not finished
  • \(p \ge 0.05\): Residuals are indistinguishable from white noise
r = resid.dropna()
p = acorr_ljungbox(r, lags=[24], return_df=True)["lb_pvalue"].iloc[0]
print(f"{len(r)} residuals, lag 24:  p = {p:.1e}")
405 residuals, lag 24:  p = 8.6e-241

Motivation from Day 1: With 24 individual lag bounds at 95%, expecting 1 spike by chance is normal. Ljung-Box pools all lags into a single test: - H_0: all autocorrelations up to lag l are zero. - Rule of thumb: l = 24 for seasonal monthly data (2m), K = 0 for benchmarks (no estimated params).

The headline is the exponent: p = 8.6e-241 (240 zeros). The best baseline is nowhere near finished white noise. Day 3 models exist to capture this remaining signal.

Four properties, and two different repairs

You just failed three of them by looking. Each failure sends you somewhere different.

Essential: fix the model

  1. Uncorrelated: fails, slow decay
    a pattern in the errors is signal the model never took
  2. Zero mean: fails, mean +7.9
    bias, in the same direction every month

Useful: fix the band

  1. Constant variance: fails, spread 8.5 then 26.2
    one spread number sizes the band for every era
  2. Normal: not yet checked
    it sets the multiplier that widens the band

The left column failed and segment A already showed the repair. The rest of this deck repairs the right column by changing the interval, not the series. fpp3’s other route, a log or Box-Cox transform, is Day 3’s.

This slide is a summary, not an introduction. Everything in the left column and item 3 on the right was derived on the previous four slides, so read the verdicts off rather than re-teaching them. The only new line is normality, and it is deliberately unmarked: segment C opens with it.

Say the split as an instruction rather than as a taxonomy. A left-column failure is a message about the model: go back and change it. A right-column failure leaves the point forecast alone and tells you the band around it is wrong. A model can pass the left column and fail the right.

The right column is worth saying out loud as a pair, because both feed one formula: the band is a spread number times a multiplier. Constant variance is what lets a single spread number stand for the whole history; normality is what makes the multiplier the right size. Fail either and the width is wrong while the centre is fine.

One honest caveat if someone asks: uncorrelated appears on both sides. It is essential for the model, and it is also one of the three assumptions behind the Gaussian interval, which segment C spells out.

Expect a stats-literate room to reach for a transform before you offer it, so have the answer ready. Taking logs, or a Box-Cox with the spine’s lambda of about 0.07, would stop the spread growing with the level and move item 3 out of the right column entirely. It is a real fix and it is deliberately not today’s: today is about what to do when the band is wrong, and Day 3 opens with the transform.

Segments C, D and E are, in order, the three ways to stop relying on the right column. Lab exercise 2.2 runs this same checklist on the drift method and asks them to write the verdict.

C. Prediction intervals

Ch 5.5 · Quantifying uncertainty

A range is what a decision can act on

Point forecast

“Demand will be 340 units.”

  • Guaranteed to be wrong
  • Hides the operational risk

Interval forecast

“Demand: [300, 380], 80% of the time.”

  • Sizes the safety stock
  • States the risk out loud

Decisions like staffing buffers, warehouse capacity, and safety inventory depend directly on the interval width.

The future is a range, not a point

At forecast origin \(T\) the future value is still random. Capital \(Y\) marks a value that has not been observed yet; lowercase \(y\) one that has:

\[Y_{T+h}\mid \mathcal{I}_T \qquad \mathcal{I}_T = \text{everything known at time } T\]

  • Forecast distribution: plausible future values, given what is known at \(T\)
  • Point forecast: one centre, usually a mean or median
  • 80% interval: 10th to 90th percentile
  • 95% interval: 2.5th to 97.5th percentile

The unknown future value, not the prediction, is represented by a distribution.

Start with what is uncertain. We produce one point forecast, but we do not believe the future must equal that point. Before it is observed, we represent the future as plausible outcomes conditional on what we know now.

The point forecast is one summary. An interval takes two quantiles. The rest of the deck asks how to construct them: impose a Gaussian shape, simulate futures from empirical errors, or calibrate a set from past forecast errors.

Gaussian intervals impose a shape

\[\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T - K - M}\sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{y}_{T+1|T} \pm c\,\hat{\sigma}\]

Residual Spread \(\hat{\sigma}\)

  • \(K\): parameters estimated (0 for benchmarks)

  • \(M\): missing residuals (e.g. 12 for seasonal naive)

  • Dividing by \(T\) makes \(\hat{\sigma}\) too small

Normal Multiplier \(c\)

  • 80% interval: \(c = 1.28\)

  • 95% interval: \(c = 1.96\)

  • Exact only if residuals are normal

The M term is where people trip: a seasonal naive cannot produce fitted values for its first 12 months, so 12 residuals are missing (405 residuals, not 417). Undercounting missing values makes sigma-hat too small and intervals too narrow.

The multiplier c assumes standard normality. If residuals have fat tails or skew, 1.28 is the wrong multiplier. Segment D (bootstrap) and Segment E (conformal) solve this.

How fast the range widens depends on the method

Benchmark \(h\)-step forecast standard deviation
Mean \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1 + 1/T}\)
Naive \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h}\)
Seasonal naive \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{k+1}\),   \(k = \lfloor (h-1)/m \rfloor\)
Drift \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h\left(1 + \dfrac{h}{T-1}\right)}\)

Seasonal naive width is a staircase: flat within each year (\(h \le 12\)), stepping up only when crossing into another full cycle (\(k+1\)).

All four formulas assume the residuals are uncorrelated. That assumption is already known to be false here - segment B just rejected it at p ~ 1e-240.

Two of the four are readable straight off their point forecasts, so read them off. The mean’s 1 + 1/T is one variance for the noise plus one T-th for not knowing the true mean. The drift’s T-1 is the T-1 gaps its slope was averaged over, the same T-1 that is in its point forecast; the more history the slope came from, the less the extrapolation adds.

If you want one sentence for this slide: the interval is the forecast, and the line down the middle is just its most likely value.

The Gaussian interval rests on three assumptions

  1. Residuals are uncorrelated: otherwise \(\hat{\sigma}\) understates the spread
  2. Residuals have constant variance: one \(\hat{\sigma}\) for the whole series
  3. Residuals are normally distributed: otherwise \(c = 1.28\) is the wrong multiplier

Segment B failed the first two by looking. The third has never been checked.

Count them off on your fingers, because the next two segments buy them back one at a time and it helps if the room is counting with you. Two of the three were measured in segment B - the slow decay, and the SD tripling across the eras. The third, normality, is the one segment B left open, and the next slide closes it. Bootstrap buys back normality. Conformal avoids specifying a distributional shape, but replaces those assumptions with exchangeability. Distribution-free never means assumption-free.

Normality fails too, and it makes the band too wide

All three assumptions fail. The next two methods each buy one of them back.

This is the check segment B put off, and it closes the loop on the four properties: normality was item 4, and it was the only one left unmarked.

Read the panels as a pair. The left one is constant variance, failing in the plainest way a picture can fail: a rolling SD that climbs while the pooled sigma-hat sits flat through the middle of it. One number is standing in for a spread that tripled, so it is too big for the 1980s and too small for the 2010s.

The right one is normality. Excess kurtosis is 0 for a normal; this is nearly 6, and the histogram shows why - a peak far sharper than the curve, and tails outside it. Then point at the two pairs of vertical lines, because that is where the abstraction turns into money. Orange is where a normal of this SD puts the central 80%; blue is where these errors actually put it. Blue is the narrower pair, about a fifth narrower, which is exactly the width segment D’s bootstrap comes back with.

Blue is also off-centre, and someone will spot it. That is the +7.9 bias again: these errors are not centred on zero, so their middle 80% is not either. A symmetric plus-or-minus cannot represent that at all.

Worth saying that the fat tails cut the other way further out: at 95% the errors’ own span is about a tenth WIDER than the normal’s. Sharp peak, fat tails, so the 80% band shrinks and the 95% band does not. That is the punchline of the bootstrap comparison two slides on, and saying it here makes it a confirmation rather than a surprise.

D. Bootstrapped intervals

Ch 5.5 · Dropping the normality assumption

Roughly 33 minutes into the deck, with about 22 still to run before the real break at the deck boundary. Take two minutes here and let the room stand up. Segments D and E are the densest stretch of Day 2, and they land better on a room that has moved.

Resample the errors you actually saw

Stop assuming a bell curve. Use the 405 errors the model already made: draw one at random, add it, step forward, repeat.

Naive: last month + an error

\(y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t\)

\[y^*_{T+1} = y_T + e^*_{T+1}\] \[y^*_{T+2} = y^*_{T+1} + e^*_{T+2}\]

Seasonal naive: last year + an error

\(y_t = y_{t-m} + e_t\)

\[y^*_{T+i} = y^*_{T+i-m} + e^*_{T+i}\]

\(e^*\): a past residual, drawn with replacement · \(y^*\): one path out to \(h\), which is one possible future

Say the idea before the algebra. Every one of these models says the next value is something we already know plus an error: e_t = y_t - yhat_{t|t-1} rearranges to y_t = yhat_{t|t-1} + e_t. Segment C filled that error in from a formula, by assuming it was normal with SD sigma-hat. We have just seen that it is not normal. So fill it in from the 405 errors the model actually made instead: put them in a hat, pull one out, write it down, put it back.

With replacement is the part to say out loud. There are 405 residuals and we want 5000 futures of 24 months each, which is 120000 draws; the same past error turns up many times, and that is fine, because we are treating those 405 as a sample of the errors this model makes rather than as a list of what will happen.

Then read the two columns as the same recipe on two different lags. The naive adds an error to last month, the seasonal naive to last year. Repeat 24 times and you have one path; the next slide runs it 5000 times.

The seeding detail is the mechanism behind the fan, and it is worth the extra sentence. The first twelve simulated months each hang off a real observed value plus one drawn error, so they scatter by about one error’s worth. Month 13 hangs off simulated month 1, so it carries that error plus a new one, and from there the errors accumulate. That is the fan, and it is why the band steps out at exactly the same place the closed-form staircase does.

The assumption you just made

Core Assumption: Identical Distribution Residuals are assumed to come from a single fixed pool \(\hat{F}\) that does not change over time (i.i.d. draws).

What you bought

  • Normality dropped: uses true error shape
  • Skew and kurtosis handled naturally

What you still owe

  • Identical distribution: fails as SD tripled
  • Pooling old and new residuals hurts coverage

The bootstrap trades normality for identical distribution. On this series that trade fails if we pool all years because residual spread tripled. Restricting the bootstrap to recent residuals (the i.i.d. knob) restores coverage from ~61% to ~87-91%.

The band is not the method. The paths are the method; the band is a percentile taken down each column.

This is the slide that fixes the most common misconception about the bootstrap. Students see the shaded band and assume the band is the method. It is not. The method is these paths; the band is a percentile taken down each column afterwards.

Trace one path with your finger from the origin out to 24 months, and say: this is one possible future, drawn by taking last year’s value and adding a real error we actually observed at some point in the past.

E. Conformal prediction

Ch 5.5 · Distribution-free intervals, and the only method that works on a model with no formula

Name the error first: \(e_{t+h|t}\)

\[e_{t+h|t} = y_{t+h} - \hat{y}_{t+h|t}\]

Indices

  • \(t\): Forecast origin (when made)
  • \(h\): Forecast horizon (steps ahead)
  • \(t+h\): Future time being predicted

Residual vs Horizon Error

  • \(h = 1\): Standard 1-step residual
  • \(h > 1\): Out-of-sample error, collected across rolling origins

Notation discipline pays off here. e_{t+h|t} carries two indices for a reason: which point is being forecast, and which origin it was forecast from. A 12-step error made in 2010 and a 12-step error made in 2016 are two members of the same calibration set.

Residuals had one index because there was only ever one origin: yesterday. For h > 1, errors must be collected, not fitted.

A worked example of \(e_{t+h|t}\)

  • Walk-through
  • Plot

You hold \(y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{10}\) and want a 3-step-ahead interval.

  1. Stand at \(t = 5\). Pretend \(y_6 \dots y_{10}\) do not exist yet.
  2. Forecast three steps out: \(\hat{y}_{8|5}\).
  3. Now look up what really happened: \(y_8\).
  4. Record \(e_{{8|5}} = y_8 - \hat{{y}}_{{8|5}}\), one 3-step-ahead error.

Slide the origin to \(t = 6, 7, \dots\) and repeat. What you collect is the calibration set for \(h = 3\).

Walk this by hand on the numbers. Stand at t=5, the naive forecast for t=8 is whatever y_5 was, then look up what actually happened at t=8 and subtract. That single number is one member of the calibration set.

Slide the origin forward and you get another. Conformal prediction is nothing more elaborate than collecting those and taking a quantile of their absolute values.

Distribution-free is not assumption-free

Core Requirement: Exchangeability Calibration errors and the next test error must be exchangeable (order invariant).

Empirical quantile of collected absolute errors: \[\hat{y}_{T+h|T} \pm Q_{1-\alpha}\big(|e_{t+h|t}|\big)\]

Per-Horizon Width One quantile per \(h\): interval growth with horizon is measured, not assumed

Distribution-Free No \(\hat{\sigma}\), no normal table, no parametric distribution required

Exchangeability asks whether the calibration errors and the next error remain comparable when their order is shuffled. It permits dependence that i.i.d. forbids, but does not excuse a changing regime (e.g. non-constant variance over decades).

Because the interval is an empirical quantile of actual model errors, there is no sigma-hat to estimate and no normal table to look up. That is why conformal intervals work on models that have no closed-form formula (ML models, neural nets, Day 3).

statsforecast does exactly this internally: n_windows rolling origins, the absolute error at each horizon, then the empirical quantile of those.

Any model at all can be given a conformal interval

from statsforecast.utils import ConformalIntervals

sf_conf = StatsForecast(
    models=[SeasonalNaive(
        season_length=12,
        prediction_intervals=ConformalIntervals(n_windows=8, h=24),
    )],
    freq="MS",
)
fc_conf = sf_conf.forecast(df=train, h=24, level=[80, 95])
  • n_windows: how many rolling origins make up the calibration set
  • Wants n_windows \(\times\,h + 1\) observations (193 here). Given fewer it silently fits as many windows as it can, and only errors below \(2h + 1\)
  • It mirrors each horizon’s 8 absolute errors to \(\hat{y} \pm |e|\) and takes the quantile of those 16 values, so the band is visibly jittery

This is the practical payoff of the whole segment and the thing a Day 3 author will need: any model that has no closed-form interval gets one from prediction_intervals=ConformalIntervals(…). Not every statsforecast model can produce intervals on its own; all of them can produce conformal ones.

The jitter warning is worth making concrete. Eight rolling origins give eight absolute errors per horizon; statsforecast reflects each one to both sides of the point forecast and takes the quantile of the sixteen values that makes, which is why the slide says sixteen where the room will have counted eight. The band wobbles because sixteen numbers is a small sample, not because anything is broken.

The sizing bullet matters more than it looks. n_windows is a request, not a setting: statsforecast quietly takes min(n_windows, (n-1)//h) and only raises if that falls below 2. Ask for 8 windows at h=24 on a series of 200 points and you get 8; on 150 you silently get 6, with no warning anywhere. Anyone taking this to Day 3 or to work should check what they actually got.

The printed numbers are the trap, so read them out and then say plainly where they came from: 24 points. The next slide is the arithmetic on why that cannot separate three methods.

Every interval method spends an assumption

Method Assumes Gives
Gaussian uncorrelated · constant variance · normal closed form
Bootstrap uncorrelated · i.i.d. from \(\hat{F}\) any error shape
Conformal exchangeable \(h\)-step errors no parametric shape

Important

Pick by which assumption you can defend, not by the widths on the last slide.

The table is the takeaway. Each row is a trade, and the honest question when choosing is not “which is most accurate” but “which of these three assumptions am I willing to make about this series”.

If the room wants a default: conformal, because it assumes least, and because it is the one that generalises to models that have no formula. That is also why it is the one Day 3 will lean on. But say the qualification out loud: its interval is distribution-free, not assumption-free, and a changing error regime can break exchangeability.

Why one test window is misleading

\[\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.8 \times 0.2}{24}} \approx 8\%\]

\[\Longrightarrow \qquad \text{95% CI on 80% coverage: } [64\%, 96\%]\]

Single Holdout (\(n = 24\)) Coverage measures 96% by chance alone. You cannot validate coverage on a single slice.

8-Fold CV (\(n = 96\))

  • Gaussian: 77%
  • Bootstrap: 61%
  • Conformal: 83%

Methods separate cleanly.

A nominal 80% interval easily measures anywhere from 64% to 96% on a single 24-point holdout by chance alone (and points clump rather than being independent coin flips).

The bootstrap’s 61% is the i.i.d. assumption being punished: pooling 1980s residuals (SD 8.5) with 2010s (SD 26.2) makes the draw pool too tight. Restricting the pool to recent residuals restores coverage to ~87-91%.

Over 8 rolling origins, we actually get enough points to evaluate reliability.
