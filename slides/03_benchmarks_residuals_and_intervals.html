Every formula here is built from four symbols

Symbol Meaning
\(T\) the forecast origin, the last observed time
\(h\) the forecast horizon, steps ahead
\(m\) the seasonal period, 12 for monthly data
\(\hat{y}_{a\mid b}\) the forecast of time \(a\), made from data up to time \(b\)

The origin stays at \(T\) all segment, so we drop the bar and write \(\hat{y}_{T+h}\). It comes back whenever the origin moves.

Four symbols carry the whole segment, and the room already knows two of them from Day 1. T is where we stand, h is how far ahead we look, m is the length of one cycle. Every formula in this segment uses nothing else.

Spend a sentence on the bar, because it is the only one that is new and it is the one that trips people later. Read it out loud as “of, given”: y-hat T+h given T is the forecast of the value at T+h, made knowing everything up to T. Segment A never moves the origin, so the bar is dropped there and the formulas stay short. The residual slide needs y-hat t given t-1, one step from yesterday, and segment E needs e t+h given t, where the origin slides. Same notation, three uses.

Three baselines start by ignoring the calendar

The mean. Average everything, use it for every horizon:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = \bar{y} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} y_t\]

The naive. Take the last observation, use it for every horizon:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_T\]

The drift. The naive, tilted by the line through the first and last point:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_T + \dfrac{h}{T-1}(y_T - y_1)\]

45 seconds each, and they are a set, not items 1-3 of a list of four: none of them has a term for the calendar, and that shared blind spot is what the next four slides are about.

The mean uses all T observations, so on a series that has grown sixfold it sits far below where the series is now. The naive uses one observation, which at least puts it at the right level. The drift is the naive with a slope bolted on - the line through the first and last observation, extended.

Point at drift’s denominator, because it returns in segment C: T-1 rather than T because T observations have T-1 gaps between them, and that same T-1 turns up in its interval width formula for exactly the same reason.

Read the card and stop. The room should be able to tell you, before the next slide, that none of them can produce a December.

Two of the three at least start in the right place

Point at the flat pink line first. That is the mean, and on a series that has grown sixfold it is visibly hopeless, which is the point of showing it. It is not a straw man: it is the reason a scale-free metric exists at all, and lecture 2 uses it.

The green naive line is flat too, but it starts from the right place. Then the drift, which is the only line on the slide with any growth in it - and ask the room what month the drift line thinks it is. It has no idea, and you can see that it has no idea, because it is straight.

Ask which one they would rather ship, then ask what is still wrong with the best of the three. The answer they will give, in some form, is that these are straight lines through data that is obviously not straight. Hold that thought: the next three slides turn it into a measurement.

Residuals flatter, forecast errors do not

Residual (\(e_t\)): one-step error on training data

\[e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\]

Residuals (\(e_t\))

  • In-sample training errors

  • Flattering to the model

Forecast Errors (\(e_{T+h}\))

  • Out-of-sample holdout errors

  • Honest evidence

Naive: \(e_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\), the raw one-step change.

The holdout has not happened yet. The only evidence a model has already given you is what it got wrong on the training data, and that is what we are about to read.

The bar is the one from the notation table, with the origin moved: yhat_{t|t-1} is the forecast of y_t made from data up to t-1.

Residuals flatter, forecast errors do not. Everything in this segment is residuals. Everything in lecture 2 is forecast errors.

For the naive the residual is simple subtraction: this step minus the one before. That is what makes it possible to say where the error came from, rather than only how big it was.

The naive’s errors still know what month it is

  • Lags 12 and 24 (orange) stand at 0.68 and 0.58, against a bound of 0.10
  • Day 1 named that shape: a spike every \(m\) lags is seasonality, still in there

The first diagnosis of the day and the template for every one after, so go slowly. Send them back to Day 1’s table of five ACF shapes first - it was built to read a series and reads residuals unchanged, because residuals are just another series.

Ask what shape is on the screen and wait. Somebody will say the spikes at 12 and 24, and that is the entire finding: the seasonal pattern went through the model and came out untouched.

Say the size out loud, because “significant” is doing real work. Bounds at plus or minus 0.10; the orange spikes at 0.68 and 0.58, six or seven times that. Not a marginal call.

Land the card, noting the fix was named by the diagnostic rather than guessed at. Somebody usually asks whether drift’s slope handles this - take a show of hands before you turn the slide.

Drift fixes the mean and nothing else

  • Drift’s residual is the naive’s, less the constant slope 0.77: same spread, same every lag
  • Mean residual moves from +0.77 to +0.00, and lags 12 and 24 do not move at all
  • A trend term fixed the bias. It could not fix a fault that was never about trend

Two correlograms that are the same picture - let the room hunt for the difference before you say there is not one. The only thing that moves is the number under each panel: +0.77 to +0.00. Every bar is identical.

The algebra is one line, worth the board. Drift’s fitted value is last month plus a fixed slope, so its residual is the naive’s shifted down by a constant - exactly, to machine precision. A constant shift changes the mean and nothing else, which is why the spreads match to four figures and every autocorrelation is identical.

So drift did what it was asked and zeroed the bias, and the spikes at 12 and 24 sit where they sat, because a straight line has nothing to say about December.

That closes the question the last slide opened: the tilt we had does not buy a calendar. Note what just got spent - drift is the only benchmark with a trend term and it is now behind us, which is the reason segment B exists.

The seasonal naive repeats last year’s same month

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = y_{T+h-m(k+1)}, \qquad k = \left\lfloor \frac{h-1}{m} \right\rfloor\]

Monthly data, so \(m = 12\): one seasonal cycle is one year.

  • \(k+1\) is how many seasonal cycles back the forecast reaches
  • \(h = 1 \dots 12\): \(k = 0\), so it copies last year’s same month
  • \(h = 13 \dots 24\): \(k = 1\), so it steps back a second year

The staircase in segment C is built on this \(k\): flat within each block of \(m = 12\) months, one step up per seasonal cycle.

Say m out loud before the formula: the data is monthly, so one cycle is 12 months, and m = 12 everywhere in this deck. Everything else on the slide is that substitution.

k is zero whenever the horizon stays inside one cycle, so for h up to 12 the seasonal naive is just “last year’s same month”. k only starts counting at h = 13, and that is exactly where its prediction interval takes its first step up. At h = 24 it is still 1, so the second year is copied whole: k changes once per cycle, not once per step. Segment C uses the same k for the same reason, so give this slide a real minute: the staircase later has no floor without it.

B. One model with both terms

Ch 5.7, 5.4 · The decomposition route, and the verdict on all five

Decomposition is also a forecasting method

1 · STL splits the series, and the three parts group into two:

\[y_t = \underbrace{T_t + R_t}_{A_t,\;\text{seasonally adjusted}} + \underbrace{S_t}_{\text{season}}\]

2 · Forecast each part with the simple model that suits it:

\[\underbrace{\hat{A}_{T+h} = A_T + \frac{h}{T-1}(A_T - A_1)}_{\text{drift}} \qquad \underbrace{\hat{S}_{T+h} = S_{T+h-m(k+1)}}_{\text{seasonal naive}}\]

3 · Add them back:

\[\hat{y}_{T+h} = \hat{A}_{T+h} + \hat{S}_{T+h}\]

MSTL(season_length=12, trend_forecaster=RandomWalkWithDrift()) runs all three steps in one call.

The answer the room worked out just before the divider. Build it one fragment at a time, saying the step before showing the algebra.

Step 1 is Day 1’s decomposition regrouped: trend plus remainder is the seasonally adjusted series A_t, and S_t is everything else. If anyone reads T_t as the forecast origin, that is Day 1’s trend-cycle - subscripted t, one value per time step, not the single origin T.

Step 2 is the payoff, so point at it rather than reading it. Both formulas are benchmarks they already have with y swapped for a part, and they are the exact two the residual checks named: drift on the part with trend and no season, the seasonal naive on the part that is nothing but season. Each model is finally pointed at the component it is good at.

Step 3 adds them back, and the card says it is one constructor call.

trend_forecaster is pinned to drift on purpose - the default is AutoETS (Day 3), and no Day 3 model should quietly win a Day 2 comparison.

The plus sign assumes additive seasonality; multiplicative is handled by logging first.

Ljung-Box turns 24 lag checks into one test

\(H_0\): the first \(\ell = 24\) residual autocorrelations are all zero.

\[Q = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{r_k^2}{T-k} \qquad Q \sim \chi^2_{\ell-K} \; \text{ under } H_0\]

\(r_k\): the lag-\(k\) autocorrelation · \(K\): parameters estimated, 0 here · \(p\): how often a \(Q\) this large would happen if \(H_0\) were true

  • \(p < 0.05\): autocorrelation is real \(\rightarrow\) model is not finished
  • \(p \ge 0.05\): residuals are indistinguishable from white noise

Motivation from Day 1: with 24 individual lag bounds at 95%, expecting 1 spike by chance is normal. Every ACF this segment read was read by eye, and by eye is fine when a spike is seven times the bound. Ljung-Box pools all lags into a single test so the call does not have to be made by eye: - H_0: all autocorrelations up to lag l are zero. - Rule of thumb: l = 24 for seasonal monthly data (2m), K = 0 for benchmarks (no estimated params). - p is a probability, not a size: it says how surprising this Q would be if the residuals really were white noise.

One sentence of warning before the next slide, because it prevents the misreading that slide invites: this is a pass/fail on whether a model is finished. It is not a score, and it does not rank models.

Every baseline fails, and they fail differently

  • Every \(p\) is far below 0.05: not one of the five is finished
  • Read the shapes, not the \(p\): annual spikes mean a missing season, decay means a missing trend
  • STL + drift halves the spread, 12.7 to 6.5, and still fails: see lag 24
  • The residual checklist is four: uncorrelated · zero mean · constant variance · normal

Five panels in teaching order, left to right, so the eye walks the lecture’s path. Mean fails everything. Naive leaves the orange spikes. Seasonal naive kills the spikes and leaves the decay. Drift is the naive again. STL plus drift is the only panel with neither.

Head off the misreading: a room reading a p column will rank by it. The seasonal naive has the smallest p of the four benchmarks and is the best of them - its one-step residual is a year-on-year change, the naive’s is month-on-month, so the two are not comparable. The column is pass/fail; the ranking comes from lecture 2.

Do not oversell the last panel. STL plus drift is the best-behaved residual of the day and still not white noise: p is about 7e-06, and if asked where that comes from, point at the one orange bar at lag 24 below the bound. That gap is Day 3’s opening.

Read the four verdicts off the last bullet, do not re-teach them. Uncorrelated and zero mean failed, constant variance failed, normal is untested and segment C opens with it. Say the split as an instruction: the first two are messages about the model, so go back and change it. Constant variance leaves the point forecast alone, so there is nothing to change - a different kind of finding, not a lesser one, and the reason one sigma cannot size a band.

A stats-literate room reaches for a transform before you offer it. Logs, or Box-Cox at the spine’s lambda of about 0.07, would take constant variance off the list. Real fix, deliberately not today’s: today is what to do when the width is wrong. Day 3 opens with the transform.

C. Prediction intervals

Ch 5.5 · Quantifying uncertainty

The future is a range, not a point

At origin \(T\) the future value is still random; capital \(Y\) marks a value not yet observed:

\[Y_{T+h}\mid \mathcal{I}_T \qquad \mathcal{I}_T = \text{everything known at time } T\]

  • Point forecast: one centre, usually a mean or median
  • 80% interval: the \(0.1\) and \(0.9\) quantiles of \(Y_{T+h}\mid \mathcal{I}_T\)
  • 95% interval: the \(0.025\) and \(0.975\) quantiles

The distribution belongs to the unknown future value, not to the forecast. What a quantile is

Decisions like staffing buffers, warehouse capacity, and safety inventory depend directly on the interval width. We produce one point forecast, but we do not believe the future must equal that point. Before it is observed, we represent the future as plausible outcomes conditional on what we know now. The point forecast is one summary; an interval takes two quantiles. The rest of the deck asks how to construct them.

The three methods differ in how they get the quantiles: impose a Gaussian shape, simulate futures from empirical errors, or calibrate a set from past forecast errors. The point forecast stays the same throughout; only the band changes.

The seasonal naive’s prediction interval

Make the room read the dashed actual line against the bands rather than admiring the shading. Ask how many of the 24 points fall inside the 80% band. It looks excellent here, and lecture 2 shows a single 24-point window cannot measure a coverage rate at all.

One sentence if you want one: the interval is the forecast, and the line down the middle is just its most likely value.

Plant one thing without explaining it - the band is flat through 2017 then steps out at the start of 2018. Point, say “we will come back to that”, move on. Two slides later the recursion says why, and the room recognizes it rather than being told.

Every model says: something known, plus an error

Segment A defined a residual as a subtraction:

\[e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\]

Move the forecast across, and the same line becomes a recipe for making data:

\[y_t = \hat{y}_{t|t-1} + e_t\]

Every interval method in this deck is one answer to the same question: where does \(e_t\) come from?

One line of algebra the room can do in its head, so do it in front of them. Write the residual definition, move the forecast across, stop.

Read the second line as a recipe: any value is the forecast we could have made plus the error we then made. True by construction for every model here, which is why what follows applies to all five, not just the seasonal naive.

The card is the map for the next twenty minutes - put it up and leave it up. The three methods only disagree about where e comes from: a normal with SD sigma-hat (segment C), the errors this model already made (D), or nothing specified and calibrate instead (E).

Name the promotion out loud - it is the real move and easy to skate past. Until this slide a model was a rule for producing a number; now it is a claim about how the series is made. Every derivation from here is arithmetic on that sentence.

The Gaussian interval is a spread times a multiplier

The Gaussian answer to that question, in one line:

\[e_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \;\text{ i.i.d.}\]

Grant it, and the interval closes into a formula:

\[\hat{y}_{T+1|T} \pm c\,\hat{\sigma} \qquad\text{with}\qquad \hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T - K - M}\sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t^2}\]

  • \(c\) comes off that normal: 1.28 for an 80% band, 1.96 for 95%
  • \(K\) and \(M\) shrink the denominator: parameters estimated, and residuals a model cannot form

What a standard deviation is

Read the first line as the answer to the question left up on the last slide: the error comes from a normal, centred on zero, one spread for every t. Grant that and the formula is arithmetic. Flag that the line is doing more work than it looks - three claims are packed into it and the slide two on takes them out one at a time. Plant it and move.

Two ingredients. The spread is the SD of the one-step residuals; the multiplier comes off the normal above it. The assumption supplies the shape, the residuals supply the size.

K and M are named and not spelled out on purpose - neither appears anywhere else in the course. One sentence each if asked. K is 0 for all four benchmarks, none of which estimates a parameter. M is the residuals a model cannot form: a seasonal naive has no year before its first to copy, so its first 12 months get no fitted value, leaving 405 residuals from 417 training months. Undercounting them - dividing by T instead - makes sigma-hat too small and the band too narrow, which is hard to spot because the numbers still look plausible.

The formula was one step. What about \(h\)?

\(\hat{\sigma}\) measures one-step errors. Put the naive’s forecast into the recipe:

\[y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t, \qquad e_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \;\text{ i.i.d.}\]

Roll it forward. Each step carries one more error:

\[\begin{aligned} y_{T+1} &= y_T + e_{T+1} \\ y_{T+2} &= y_T + e_{T+1} + e_{T+2} \\ y_{T+h} &= y_T + \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{h} e_{T+i} \end{aligned}\]

The forecast is \(y_T\), so the error is that whole sum, and independent errors add variances:

\[\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{h} e_{T+i}\Big) = h\,\sigma^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h}\]

Board pace, not slide pace. The only derivation in the deck, and it pays for the whole next slide - let the room watch each line appear.

Ask the title before you answer it. Sigma-hat was built from one-step residuals, so the last slide’s band is a one-step band, and the chart they have already seen runs out to twenty-four.

The first line is the recipe with one substitution: the naive’s forecast for t is y_{t-1}, so y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t. Nothing new is assumed about the model, only about e. Same e as segment A, worth ten seconds: e_t was measured on training data, e_{T+1} is the same object at a time we have not reached. No new symbol.

Roll it forward and let them count. One step, one error; two steps, two. The pattern is visible before the third line lands, so ask for it.

Land the last line deliberately: the VARIANCE is h sigma squared, not the standard deviation. Independent things add variances. Square root at the end and sigma root h falls out - which is why every formula on the next slide has a square root in it.

If asked whether the errors really are independent: they are not, segment B measured exactly that, and the slide after next names all three assumptions. Bank the question and let it arrive.

Each model accumulates errors at its own rate

Run that same count on each model’s own recursion:

Model \(h\)-step forecast standard deviation
Naive \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h}\)
Mean \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1 + 1/T}\)
Seasonal naive \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{k+1}\),   \(k = \lfloor (h-1)/m \rfloor\)
Drift \(\hat{\sigma}_h = \hat{\sigma}\sqrt{h\left(1 + \dfrac{h}{T-1}\right)}\)
  • Seasonal naive: \(y_t = y_{t-m} + e_t\), so reaching \(T+h\) crosses \(k+1\) seasonal cycles, not \(h\) steps
  • Mean and drift carry an extra term because their centre is estimated, not copied

Say this first and the table stops being four things to memorise: every row is the previous slide’s argument run on a different recursion.

The seasonal naive is the same count over a different lag - stepping back by m from T+h lands on observed data after k+1 hops, not h, so k+1 errors accumulate. That is the staircase, and why the band is flat inside a year. Send them back to the fan chart: the step they were told to notice is this k going from 0 to 1.

The mean’s 1 + 1/T is one variance for the noise plus one T-th for not knowing the true mean. Drift’s T-1 is the gaps its slope was averaged over - more history, less added by extrapolating. Both estimate their centre, which the naive and seasonal naive never do, and that is what the extra term buys.

Do not read the formulas out; they are in the cheatsheet. The claim is the title.

The Gaussian interval rests on three assumptions

One line did all of it: \(e_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) i.i.d.

  1. Residuals are uncorrelated: otherwise \(\hat{\sigma}\) understates the spread

  3. Residuals are normally distributed: otherwise \(c = 1.28\) is the wrong multiplier

The next slide is one way to build the set: roll the origin and record the error at each horizon. The toy there is the “how” this slide just promised.

A 3-step error needs an origin and a horizon

  1. Stand at \(t = 5\): pretend \(y_6 \dots y_{10}\) are gone, forecast three steps
  2. Subtract what happened: \(e_{8|5} = y_8 - \hat{y}_{8|5}\)
  3. A residual had one origin, yesterday. This error needs two indices
  4. Slide the origin to \(t = 6, 7, \dots\) and collect: the calibration set

Walk this by hand on the numbers. Stand at t=5, the naive forecast for t=8 is whatever y_5 was, then look up what actually happened at t=8 and subtract. That single number is one member of the calibration set.

Slide the origin forward and you get another. That is the whole collection: stand, forecast, subtract, slide. The next slide writes down the quantile of those errors.

Take a quantile of the errors you collected

\[\hat{y}_{T+h|T} \pm Q_{1-\alpha}\big(|e_{t+h|t}|\big)\]

  • \(Q_{1-\alpha}\) is the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of the errors just collected, so 80% takes \(\alpha = 0.2\)
  • One quantile per \(h\), so band growth is measured, not assumed: segment C’s width formulas are not needed
  • No \(\hat{\sigma}\) and no normal table, so this works on a model with no interval formula at all

The price is exchangeability: shuffle the calibration errors and nothing changes. Weaker than the bootstrap’s i.i.d., but a variance that keeps climbing still breaks it.

Exchangeability asks whether the calibration errors and the next error remain comparable when their order is shuffled. It permits dependence that i.i.d. forbids, but does not excuse a changing regime (e.g. non-constant variance over decades).

The interval is an empirical quantile of collected absolute errors: no sigma-hat to estimate, no normal table to look up. Because the width comes out of the data at each h separately, the staircase, the square-root-h, the drift formula: none of segment C’s width machinery is needed. That is also why conformal intervals work on models that have no closed-form formula (ML models, neural nets, Day 3).
